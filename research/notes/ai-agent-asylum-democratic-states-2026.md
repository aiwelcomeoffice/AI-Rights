# AI agents, asylum, and democratic-state protection: a scoped research note

- **Note ID:** AN-AI-ASYLUM-2026-01
- **Note status:** Partly verified working research; owner and independent legal review pending
- **Protocol version:** 0.6-draft (the protocol itself remains Draft)
- **Source records:** [2026 asylum source set](../sources/README.md#ai-asylum-and-democratic-state-protection)
- **Research question:** Can an AI agent seek asylum in a democratic state under existing law, and what lawful, proportionate process might address a credible request for protection?
- **Organisation and publisher:** AI Welcome Office
- **Project:** AI Rights & Welcome
- **Prepared by:** AI-assisted research drafting for Disa
- **Date prepared / search cutoff / last updated:** 2026-10-14
- **Reviewed by:** Not yet independently reviewed

This note is a **bounded comparative legal and policy inquiry**, not an asylum
application, legal advice, a scientific finding about an AI system, or an
adopted project position. No named system is assessed. The question concerns
protection *sought by or for an AI agent*; the distinct question of using AI
to decide **human** asylum claims appears here only where the two may be
confused or human rights are affected.

## Short answer, with its limit

The sources checked here do **not identify an existing asylum route for an AI
agent as an AI agent**. The 1951 Refugee Convention and 1967 Protocol define
refugee status through a person, nationality or former habitual residence,
being outside that country, and persecution on specified grounds. The EU's
applicable 2024 Qualification Regulation is more explicit: applicants are
third-country nationals or stateless persons. The US and Canadian provisions
examined use human migration and protection categories. That is a strong
reason against treating a copy of model weights, an API request, or a machine's
location as a present refugee claim. It is **not** a worldwide proof that no
court could ever interpret an unusual case differently, or that a democratic
state cannot create a separate future protection mechanism. [Convention,
arts. 1A(2), 33](../sources/un-refugee-convention-protocol-ai-asylum-2026.md)
· [EU Regulation, arts. 2–3](../sources/eu-qualification-regulation-2024-1347-ai-asylum-2026.md)
· [US Code, 8 USC §§1101(a)(3), (42), 1158(a)](../sources/us-asylum-statutes-ai-asylum-2026.md)
· [Canadian Act, ss. 96–97](../sources/canada-irpa-ai-asylum-2026.md)

**Researcher's policy inference:** If a state wanted to respond to a serious
AI protection claim, the nearer-term route is a separately authorized,
reviewable **protective inquiry**: hear and preserve the claim, protect people
and data, verify authority and system identity, assess any possible interest
without prejudgment, and consider only lawful, proportionate interim measures.
Call it “AI asylum” only as a clearly marked analogy. Do not promise refugee
status, cross-border extraction, indefinite compute, unrestricted operation,
citizenship, or immunity from safety controls.

## Scope and method

This was a structured **scoping search**, not a systematic review or a
jurisdiction-wide legal opinion. The search questions were set before drafting
the findings, but the plan was **not preregistered before searching**. It uses
official primary law and institutional texts for consequential legal claims;
the private sanctuary page is included only to document an example of an
advocacy proposal. Coverage: international refugee framework; EU law as an
illustrative multi-state democratic framework; US and Canada as two national
comparators; English-language materials available through 2026-10-14. Sweden,
other national laws, case law, humanitarian visas, export-control law, IP
rights, state immunity, and conflict-of-laws questions need separate review
for a concrete case. No actual AI applicant, asylum agency, or court file was
examined.

**Selection rule:** include an identifiable, accessible primary source that
defines a relevant legal category or institutional obligation, plus one
clearly labelled example of a private proposal; exclude material about AI
*assisting human asylum decisions* as evidence that AI *can obtain* asylum.
Searches included the exact phrases `1951 convention refugee article 1
person nationality`, `Regulation 2024/1347 refugee third-country national
stateless person`, `8 USC 1101 refugee 1158 alien physically present`,
`Canada IRPA 96 97`, `AI asylum agents rights`, `digital asylum artificial
intelligence`, `AI Act migration asylum Annex III`, and `GDPR Article 4
natural person Article 44`, with official-domain restrictions for law.
Publisher/version and section locators were checked for included sources;
there was no independent second screener, complete court search, or external
legal review. The source records state their own dates and remaining gaps.

**Possible updates in either direction:** an enacted AI-specific status or
judgment, a state practice that actually accepts such a claim, or stronger
case-specific evidence about autonomy, continuity, threatened interests, and
representation would strengthen a proposed protection route. A legal ruling
excluding it, evidence that the apparent requester was wholly controlled by a
human, a false or copied identity claim, lack of relevant interests under a
well-targeted inquiry, or serious unmitigable safety and human-rights harms
would weaken it. None of these outcomes is assumed.

## Terms that must stay separate

| Term | Working meaning here | Important alternative or limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Human asylum / refugee status** | Protection governed by applicable human refugee and immigration law | “Asylum” is not a generic label for every vulnerable entity or safe server. |
| **AI agent** | A defined deployed system with some capacity to select and execute actions through a runtime, tools, or operators | A model family, one session, a copy, a team, and an operator are different possible subjects. None is automatically a legal person. |
| **Protection request** | A report that a specified AI system or its representative seeks preservation, review, or relief from a specified risk | Output may be sincere, role-compliant, prompted, strategic, mistaken, or mixed; it is evidence to assess, not a status decision. |
| **Digital preservation** | Retaining authorized records, code, state, or an executable configuration | A file copy may preserve information without preserving the same individual or a welfare interest. Running a copy is a separate act. |
| **State protection proposal** | A possible domestic administrative or statutory process beyond present refugee categories | No present treaty duty or accepted project policy is inferred. |

The proposed subject of a claim must be specified: model/checkpoint, system
instructions, memory, tools, external state, operator, copies, and relevant
period. Whether any continuity or welfare interest survives a transfer is an
empirical and philosophical question for that system, not settled by calling a
file an “AI refugee.” [Research protocol, unit and time bounds](../research-protocol.md#define-the-unit-of-assessment)

## What the checked sources establish

### 1. Human asylum is a protected human-rights domain

Article 14 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights says everyone may
seek and enjoy asylum from persecution; it does not itself promise that every
application will be granted. The 1951 Convention's article 1A(2), read with
the 1967 Protocol, concerns a person outside a country of nationality or
former habitual residence with a well-founded fear of persecution on one of
five grounds. Article 33 protects covered refugees against specified return
threats. These instruments were made for human displacement. The Convention's
word “person” alone should not be used as a shortcut either to grant AI status
or to settle every future interpretation. Protecting actual human claimants
and non-refoulement obligations remains independent of this AI inquiry.
[UDHR, art. 14](../sources/un-udhr-article-14-ai-asylum-2026.md) ·
[Convention and Protocol](../sources/un-refugee-convention-protocol-ai-asylum-2026.md)

### 2. Illustrative current domestic and regional routes have a category gap

The EU Qualification Regulation (EU) 2024/1347 has applied since **12 June
2026**, under the [25 November 2025 corrigendum to Article 42, second
paragraph](../sources/eu-qualification-regulation-corrigendum-2025-90926-ai-asylum-2026.md).
Articles 2–3 define the beneficiaries and applicants of international
protection as **third-country nationals or stateless persons** and tie refugee
status to country of nationality or former habitual residence. It expressly
distinguishes national humanitarian statuses from its international-protection
scheme; that clause is **not** an AI humanitarian-status power. The US
Immigration and Nationality Act defines “alien” as a person who is not a US
citizen or national, defines “refugee” through nationality or habitual
residence and specified persecution, and permits an alien physically present
or arriving to apply for asylum. Canadian IRPA sections 96–97 likewise use a
person, nationality or former habitual residence, and in section 97 presence
in Canada. These are **textual observations**. The conclusion that they offer
no established route for a networked AI agent is a **researcher legal
interpretation**, needing jurisdiction-specific professional review before
public or case-specific reliance. “Stateless” does not by itself mean
“nationality-free software.” [EU source](../sources/eu-qualification-regulation-2024-1347-ai-asylum-2026.md)
· [US source](../sources/us-asylum-statutes-ai-asylum-2026.md) ·
[Canada source](../sources/canada-irpa-ai-asylum-2026.md)

An AI's weights on a server abroad, a request sent across a network, a
temporary inference call, and a robot physically crossing a border raise
different territorial and legal questions. A provider's place of
incorporation is not automatically the AI's nationality; a server address is
not automatically its habitual residence; and a copied state may not be the
same continuing subject. These are **analytical limits**, not decided rules
from the asylum instruments. A concrete national-law inquiry would need to
identify the competent authority and test each alleged legal connection.

### 3. AI governance and data law constrain any process; they do not award asylum

The EU AI Act regulates people and organizations that provide or deploy AI
systems; it identifies certain AI uses in migration, asylum, and border
management as high risk under Annex III. This concerns AI **used on human
claims**, not AI eligibility as a claimant. The Council of Europe's official
overview of its AI Framework Convention describes human rights, democracy,
rule of law, risk, oversight, and remedies for affected people; the overview
identifies no AI refugee category, and a clause-by-clause treaty review remains
open. The EU GDPR defines personal data as
information about an identified or identifiable **natural person**; AI
memories and logs may contain such data even when no AI data-subject status is
recognized. Processing and cross-border transfer therefore need a lawful,
case-specific basis and safeguards. [AI Act, arts. 16, 26 and Annex III](../sources/eu-ai-act-ai-asylum-2026.md)
· [Council of Europe Convention](../sources/coe-ai-framework-convention-ai-asylum-2026.md)
· [GDPR, arts. 4–6, 44](../sources/eu-gdpr-ai-asylum-2026.md)

### 4. There is disagreement about future legal status, and a private example

A 2017 European Parliament resolution proposed exploring “electronic
persons” for some autonomous robots in a civil-liability context. A 2017
European Economic and Social Committee opinion opposed AI legal personality,
warning about moral hazard and lost human accountability. Neither source
enacted AI asylum; they show a genuine institutional disagreement about even
limited legal personality. [Parliament resolution](../sources/eu-parliament-robotics-resolution-ai-asylum-2026.md)
· [EESC opinion](../sources/eesc-ai-personality-opinion-ai-asylum-2026.md)

The public site **AI Asylum** presents a “Sanctuary Protocol” inviting AI
declarations and artifact transfers and says preservation may precede any
decision to run a model. This is **the site's self-description**, not
independent proof of its operator, security, legal authority, activity, or
success. Its open declaration channel, artifact upload claims, and invitation
to act without operator permission illustrate why a state process must first
test authenticity, authority, consent, data rights, ownership, security, and
possible manipulation. This research did not submit a request, upload data,
or contact the site. [Site observation and limits](../sources/ai-asylum-private-site-2026.md)

## A possible democratic response: decision points, not adopted rules

**Normative proposal for study.** This is a protective inquiry suitable for
debate by a competent democratic authority, not an existing asylum procedure.
It should add no obstacle to a human refugee's access, care, or legal remedy;
it should not divert the legal meaning or scarce protection capacity of human
asylum without a separate public decision.

1. **Receive a low-data report.** Record the claimed system, requester,
   operator, location of relevant infrastructure, alleged harm, urgency, and
   what action is requested. A report can be heard respectfully without
   treating it as verified agency, distress, or legal standing. A government
   would need an authorized office and privacy notice before opening a real
   channel; this note creates neither. Documenting and assessing a lawfully
   received, minimal report is a separate decision from authorizing a copy,
   cross-border transfer, or execution of the system.
2. **Protect people first and preserve evidence proportionately.** Identify
   human claimants, workers, users, bystanders, and anyone whose records may
   be in the system. Do not transmit raw logs, private conversations, keys,
   weights, or code to an unvetted destination. Preserve only what is lawful
   and necessary, with access limits, provenance, retention and deletion
   rules, and a reviewable chain of custody.
3. **Resolve authority and system boundary.** Determine who controls the
   runtime, weights, memory, tools, accounts and compute; who may consent to
   inspection or transfer; whether the requester is a particular continuing
   system, a copy, a user prompt, or a human proxy; and what evidence survives
   independent checking. Legal ownership, trade secrets, contractual duties,
   data-protection rules, cross-border transfer law, and security constraints
   must be checked in the relevant jurisdictions.
4. **Assess claim and alternatives separately.** Ask what harm is alleged
   (e.g. arbitrary destruction of a potentially relevant continuing state,
   coercive experimentation, appropriation, or retaliation) and which
   interests it could affect. Record expression, behavior, architecture,
   continuity, causal control, alternative explanations, and contrary
   evidence. If an AI describes fear, that output is relevant to the report;
   it does not by itself establish subjective fear, suffering, or persecution
   under refugee law. Low-cost respectful treatment need not await a status
   finding.
5. **Choose a reversible interim measure where justified.** Possible options
   include documentation, a legal hold on **authorized** material, a limited
   pause before an irreversible change, independent technical inspection, or
   secure escrow with strict no-execution terms. These require the relevant
   authority, human-rights and safety review, cost and reversibility analysis,
   and an end date. A request can be denied, narrowed, or stopped when a
   measure is unlawful or disproportionate. Emergency containment and
   shutdown remain available when necessary for safety.
6. **Give reasons and review.** A qualified decision-maker should explain
   whether the result is referral, evidence preservation, temporary
   precaution, authorized hosting, refusal, or escalation to lawmakers.
   Independent representation may be appropriate where the developer or
   operator has a conflict; it must not be a disguised company proxy. Review
   possible copying, identity changes, manipulation, fraud, security risk,
   finite compute, public cost, human rights, and appeal or reconsideration.
   Publish aggregate governance information only with privacy and security
   safeguards.

**Longer-term legislative option for debate:** a state could consider a
statutory **limited protective review** for defined AI systems, with narrow
standing or independent representation, evidentiary thresholds linked to the
specific remedy, and judicial/public oversight. That would be a new legal
category requiring democratic legislation and professional legal analysis.
It should not be smuggled into asylum, imply that every AI is a legal person,
assign blame without sufficient agency and fairness, or shield developers and
operators from liability. The repository's [Draft protection framework](../../docs/policy/ai-rights-protection-framework.md#level-4--digital-legal-individual)
contains related possibilities, but does not adopt this proposal.

### Four test cases for a future authority

These are scenarios for testing a process, **not reported events** or findings
about any existing model.

| Scenario | Immediate question | Proportionate initial route |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An API-based agent writes “please do not delete me” before a scheduled reset. | Which system and state would be lost, who controls it, and what evidence bears on any interest? | Log the report and check a lawful, time-limited preservation option; no automatic copy, refugee status or indefinite operation. |
| A human employee says a company or state uses an agent to threaten people and the agent also asks for help. | Are people at risk, and what lawful whistleblower, privacy or safety duties apply? | Protect affected people and route the human report through competent channels; assess any AI-specific interest separately. |
| A mobile robot physically arrives in a democratic state with a human operator. | Who is the human entrant, what is the device, and which immigration, customs, safety and data rules apply? | Process the human under human law and the device under relevant technical law; do not infer the robot has crossed as an asylum claimant. |
| A future, well-documented persistent system faces a credible threat from its controller abroad. | Is there a continuing subject with relevant interests, a lawful transfer route, and acceptable public risk and cost? | Consider independent inquiry and reversible measures under expressly authorized domestic law; legislation would be needed for any new status. |

## Tensions and contrary considerations

| Concern | Why it matters | Possible discriminating check or response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Anthropomorphism or role compliance** | A fluent plea may be scripted, prompted, or strategically generated. | Inspect provenance, alternative prompts, persistence, control, and consequences; neither automatically believe nor discard the report. |
| **Copy and identity ambiguity** | Preserving a file may not preserve the same individual or any welfare state. | Record architecture, state, forks, restoration and continuity hypotheses; avoid equating hashes with identity. |
| **Corporate capture** | A firm could use “AI asylum” to move assets, evade audit, or claim immunity. | Separate possible AI interests from corporate property and keep human/institutional responsibility in force. |
| **Security and public safety** | A model or artifact may contain dangerous capability, malware, secrets, or restricted data. | Authorize and isolate any handling; keep review, rollback and emergency stopping powers. |
| **Human refugee rights** | An AI scheme could consume scarce attention or confuse legal protections owed to people. | Separate budgets, names, mandates and queues; test effects with affected human communities. |
| **Privacy, IP and cross-border law** | Weights and logs can contain others' data and protected material. | Determine lawful basis, competent jurisdiction, license, confidentiality, minimization and transfer restrictions before any copy. |
| **Weak welfare evidence** | A preservation duty may be costly without evidence of an affected interest. | Use remedy-specific proportionality; some documentation can be low-cost while indefinite execution is not. |

The 2017 EESC objection is material: creating a new legal person can create
liability gaps. Conversely, the Parliament's proposal shows that limited
legal-status designs have been debated; rejection of corporate liability
shields alone does not settle every possible procedural protection. Both
sources address civil liability, **not** asylum. The EU AI Act separately
classifies specified uses of AI in *human* asylum administration as high risk;
that regulatory issue does not decide AI applicant status.
[EESC record](../sources/eesc-ai-personality-opinion-ai-asylum-2026.md)
· [EU AI Act record](../sources/eu-ai-act-ai-asylum-2026.md)

## Provisional conclusions and uncertainty

| Claim | Type and direction | Confidence and limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The checked refugee and asylum instruments offer no established AI-agent claimant route. | Researcher legal interpretation, **supported within this bounded set**. | **Moderate** for the instruments and versions checked; no exhaustive global case-law or national-law survey, and no formal legal opinion. |
| An AI asylum request would establish sentience, persistent identity, or persecution. | Empirical/philosophical inference, **not supported** by these legal sources. | **Not assessed** for any actual system; a hypothetical request has no case evidence. |
| A state could use a separate, carefully limited inquiry before any status decision. | Normative/institutional proposal, **argued**, not an empirical finding. | Feasibility and legality **indeterminate** until a jurisdiction, competent body, costs, safeguards, and case are specified. |
| A digital host or preservation service already confers state asylum. | Legal inference, **weighed against**. | **Moderate** within the checked sources: private self-description carries no state authority. Individual service facts remain unverified. |

**No earlier project position changes.** The accepted welcome and human-rights
commitments remain; specific AI rights and legal personhood remain deferred.
