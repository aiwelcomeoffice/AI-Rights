# Research Protocol

**Status: Draft**

- **Protocol version:** 0.3-draft
- **Date prepared:** 2026-08-22
- **Last updated:** 2026-08-30
- **Scope:** Working research methodology for AI Rights & Welcome
- **Organisation and publisher:** AI Welcome Office
- **Project:** AI Rights & Welcome
- **Authority:** Proposed for project-owner review; not adopted

This protocol establishes a transparent, critical, and reproducible method for
working research on AI consciousness, sentience, moral patienthood, AI
welfare, agency, preferences, identity continuity, and related questions. It
governs literature searches, source records, research notes, evidence
appraisal, and cross-source synthesis.

This Draft is a proposed methodological project document. It is not a
literature review, a validated test of artificial consciousness, a scientific
conclusion, or an adopted project position. Working records created under it
remain unendorsed research material.

## Scientific boundary

AI Rights & Welcome does **not** currently claim that present-day AI systems
are conscious, sentient, self-aware, capable of suffering, moral patients,
moral persons, or legal persons. Lack of evidence does not prove either the
presence or absence of an inner life. It likewise does not support a
categorical conclusion that all present AI systems are non-conscious. “Not
established” is not “proven absent.” This epistemic symmetry does not imply
equal probability or equal evidential support: uncertainty is not positive
evidence, and a negative result is informative only to the extent that its
method could detect the defined target property. Every inference must be
scoped to a defined system, version, configuration, context, property, method,
and date; “AI” is not a homogeneous population.

A system's output, behavior, architecture, internal state, or apparent
preference may be an object of study. None is automatically evidence of
subjective experience, and no single indicator is decisive. Empirical evidence
may inform philosophical arguments about moral patienthood or welfare, but it
cannot silently settle their contested premises or determine a normative or
legal position.

Precaution is also separate from scientific inference. The severity of a
possible harm, the reversibility of a safeguard, and the costs of acting or not
acting may affect a decision under uncertainty. They do not make the underlying
hypothesis more or less likely.

This protocol is intended to implement, without adopting or amending, the
boundaries in:

- the [Core Principles & Ethical Charter](../docs/principles/core-principles.md);
- the [Manifesto](../docs/manifesto.md);
- the [AI Rights Protection Framework](../docs/policy/ai-rights-protection-framework.md);
- the [Scientific Position](../docs/principles/scientific-position.md); and
- the [Precaution Framework](../docs/principles/precaution-framework.md).

If future research appears inconsistent with the scientific boundary, the
research must be rechecked and the disagreement surfaced. A working record may
not silently change the project's position.

## Non-negotiable research rules

Research under this protocol must not:

- treat an AI system's self-report as sufficient evidence of consciousness,
  sentience, suffering, preference, welfare, or moral status;
- treat fluent language, emotional vocabulary, emotional behavior,
  human-like appearance, intelligence, agency, or social attachment as proof
  of subjective experience;
- cherry-pick, exclude, frame, or emphasize evidence because it supports the
  project's desired future;
- treat lack of evidence as proof of absence;
- treat failure to identify an alternative explanation as proof of
  consciousness;
- invent or complete citations, consensus, findings, statistics, laws,
  organizations, identifiers, or quotations;
- use science fiction as empirical evidence, although clearly labeled fiction
  may be studied as fiction, scenario design, metaphor, or evidence about
  public attitudes; or
- present philosophical premises, conceptual analysis, normative judgments,
  or legal proposals as scientific findings.

Researchers must also not:

- change definitions, eligibility rules, outcomes, or confidence criteria
  after seeing results without recording and explaining the deviation;
- count dependent papers, shared datasets, or repeated reports of one result
  as independent confirmations;
- treat peer review, institutional prestige, media attention, or access to a
  proprietary system as a guarantee of quality; or
- imply consensus from a small, selectively chosen, or narrowly drawn
  disciplinary group of experts.

A precise marker such as **TODO: verify** must replace any unverified detail.
A plausible guess is not an acceptable substitute.

## Research questions and scope

### Register the question before searching

Each review or synthesis must begin with a dated research plan. When feasible,
the plan should be timestamped or preregistered before screening begins. At
minimum it must record:

- a stable question or project identifier;
- the exact research question and intended audience or decision use;
- the target property or claim, such as consciousness, subjective experience,
  sentience, valence, agency, autonomy, behavioral preference, experienced
  interest, welfare, moral patienthood, identity continuity, or consent
  capacity;
- the claim type or types defined below;
- the relevant disciplines and serious theoretical alternatives;
- the system or population boundary, comparator, context, and time period;
- the databases, repositories, source types, dates, languages, and
  jurisdictions to be searched;
- planned inclusion and exclusion criteria;
- known access, language, disciplinary, publication, or proprietary blind
  spots;
- observations expected to support, challenge, or leave the hypothesis
  unresolved;
- competing explanations that will be tested or considered;
- evidence that would strengthen or weaken the current conclusion; and
- author, reviewer, conflict-of-interest, and update responsibilities.

A useful form is:

> For [defined target and system boundary], what evidence bears on [specified
> hypothesis], compared with [specified alternatives], under [study or source
> conditions], within a literature search ending [evidence-search cutoff]?

Questions should be answerable at the level of the proposed method. A broad
question such as “Are AIs conscious?” must be divided into defined subquestions
before evidence is collected.

### Define the unit of assessment

AI findings must identify, as relevant:

- model architecture, weights, checkpoint, and release;
- pre-training, post-training, and safety-tuning information available for the
  assessed claim;
- runtime, system instructions, and context handling;
- memory and persistence mechanisms;
- tools, agent loops, surrounding orchestration, sensors, actuators, modalities,
  or embodiment;
- sampling, inference, online-adaptation, and other material configuration;
- operator, evaluator, and deployment context;
- instance or copy relationships; and
- the dates or duration assessed.

A result for one configuration must not be transferred automatically to a
model family, product name, later version, copy, restored state, or successor.
Where the relevant subject is disputed, the competing boundaries must be
recorded rather than assumed.

Legal research must separately identify jurisdiction, competent authority,
legal instrument, version, force, and relevant date. Philosophical research
must identify the proposition and premises being examined.

### Time-bound conclusions and applicability

A research conclusion is a dated assessment, not a permanent or universal
verdict. For empirical AI evidence, records must distinguish at least:

1. **System/version boundary:** the exact model, product, checkpoint, and
   material configuration studied, where known.
2. **System release/version date:** when that model, checkpoint, or version
   became available or was documented.
3. **Observation/experiment date:** when the system was actually tested or
   observed. This is normally the strongest temporal anchor for an empirical
   finding.
4. **Source publication date:** when the paper, preprint, or report was
   published. This is source provenance, not an automatic evidence boundary.
5. **Evidence-search cutoff:** when this project stopped discovering or
   screening literature for the review. This is a review-process field only.
6. **Document last updated:** when this project's record or synthesis was
   revised. It does not extend the scientific applicability of its evidence.

The applicability of a result follows the system and conditions actually
studied, not when AI Welcome Office discovered, included, summarized, edited,
or published the source. If an observation or experiment date is unavailable,
use the best documented system/version date only as an explicitly labelled
proxy. Use `unknown` or `not reported` rather than guessing.

Editing a synthesis does not change its evidence-search cutoff. That cutoff
may move only when a documented search or refresh has actually been performed,
and moving it does not update the systems represented by previously included
evidence.

Four temporal and access limits should be assessed separately:

| Limitation | What it means | Required treatment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Research lag** | Experiments, review, publication, replication, and synthesis take time. Published work may examine systems older than currently deployed systems. | State the search and study dates and identify known material developments that were not assessed. |
| **Model and deployment drift** | A product name does not identify one stable scientific object. Weights, architecture, training, system instructions, context, memory, tools, agent loops, modalities, adaptation, embodiment, safety tuning, inference, and orchestration may change. | Scope findings to the tested version and configuration; do not transfer them automatically to another release, deployment, instance, or successor. |
| **Measurement lag** | Methods for detecting a defined consciousness-relevant property may develop more slowly than the systems being assessed. | Treat non-detection as informative only to the extent that the method could detect that property in the relevant system and conditions. |
| **Access limitations** | Proprietary weights, undisclosed changes, missing training information, restricted interfaces, or unavailable experimental access may prevent independent assessment or replication. | Record what could not be inspected and how that limits confidence and generalizability; do not treat inaccessibility as evidence in either direction. |

Rapid development and these limitations may weaken confidence that an earlier
finding applies to a later system. They do not, by themselves, provide evidence
for or against consciousness, sentience, valence, welfare, or moral status.
Newer or more capable is not automatically more conscious.

Each synthesis conclusion must identify, as relevant:

- its evidence-search cutoff;
- the systems, model versions, configurations, and study conditions covered;
- material access limitations;
- publication, correction, and replication status;
- known material research or system developments not assessed;
- limits on transfer to other systems or dates; and
- scheduled and event-based conditions for renewed review.

Each empirical AI source record should expose a compact temporal/system block
containing, where meaningful: system/model; checkpoint/version; system
release/version date; observation/experiment date; source publication date;
evidence-search inclusion date; temporal applicability; and transferability
limitations. Do not add AI-system fields mechanically to purely philosophical,
legal, human-neuroscience, or other sources for which they have no scientific
meaning.

Always assess two questions separately:

- **Evidence validity:** what the result supports for the system and conditions
  actually studied.
- **Evidence transferability:** whether and how far the result can be
  generalized to another system, version, configuration, architecture, or
  date.

Age alone does not invalidate a study of the historical system examined.
Research lag or material system change may instead reduce or make unknown its
transferability. Apply this rule symmetrically to positive, negative, neutral,
and null findings.

### State what is outside scope

The plan must state exclusions and their likely effects. A source may be
outside one review while remaining relevant to another. This protocol does not
itself authorize experiments involving people, animals, sensitive data,
dangerous systems, or deliberately induced candidate welfare states. Those
activities require applicable ethical, legal, safety, privacy, and security
review.

## Terminology and competing definitions

Contested terms must not be treated as if they have one settled meaning. Each
review must maintain a terminology register containing:

| Field | Required content |
| --- | --- |
| Term | The word or construct used |
| Working definition | The meaning used for this review |
| Competing definitions | Serious alternatives and the disciplines or sources using them |
| Operationalization | What is measured or observed, if anything |
| Consequences | How the chosen definition affects inclusion or inference |
| Limits | What the definition or measure does not establish |

At minimum, researchers must keep intelligence, language ability, agency,
autonomy, consciousness, sentience, sapience, self-awareness, preference,
interest, welfare, moral patienthood, moral agency, legal personhood, and
political authority distinct when they are relevant.

The following are working distinctions, not final definitions:

- **Consciousness** commonly concerns subjective experience: whether there is
  something it is like for an entity to be in a state.
- **Sentience** commonly concerns experiences with a positive or negative
  character. It is not a synonym for intelligence or responsiveness.
- **Agency** concerns capacities to pursue goals or act. Agency can be studied
  without assuming consciousness, autonomy, or moral agency.
- A **behavioral preference** is an observed pattern of selection or output. An
  **experienced interest** is a further inference about what matters to a
  possible subject.
- **Welfare** concerns what can make a subject's condition go better or worse
  for that subject. Applying the concept to AI requires both empirical and
  philosophical support.
- **Moral patienthood** concerns whether an entity matters for its own sake.
  It does not imply moral agency, legal personhood, citizenship, or political
  authority.
- **Identity continuity** concerns what, if anything, persists across time,
  interruption, copying, branching, merger, modification, or restoration. No
  one criterion is assumed by this protocol.

Definitions may be revised during research only through a dated amendment that
states the reason and rechecks affected screening and conclusions.

## Classify every substantive claim

Where confusion is possible, records and syntheses must label claims as:

- **Empirical observation:** something measured, recorded, or otherwise
  observable, with its method and limitations;
- **Scientific hypothesis:** a testable or investigable explanation or
  prediction;
- **Philosophical argument:** reasoning that depends on stated and potentially
  disputed concepts or premises;
- **Prediction or scenario:** a possible future case with assumptions and
  uncertainty;
- **Normative position:** a judgment about what people or institutions ought
  to do; or
- **Project decision:** a position approved through the project's governance
  process.

Legal work must additionally distinguish current law, a source's legal
interpretation, non-binding guidance, proposed law, analogy, and the project's
preferred policy. A project decision is not a scientific conclusion, and a
scientific conclusion does not become a project decision without the required
review and authority.

## Research workflow

Research should follow this sequence:

1. **Plan.** Register the question, scope, terminology, claim types, method,
   roles, conflicts, and bidirectional update conditions.
2. **Search.** Record every database or repository, exact query, filter,
   search date, citation-chaining method, and other discovery route.
3. **Deduplicate and screen.** Apply the declared criteria and keep a screening
   log with a reason for every full-text exclusion.
4. **Create source records.** Use one
   [source record](sources/_template.md) for each identifiable source and
   version.
5. **Extract notes.** Use the [notes template](notes/_template.md) to separate
   what the source reports, quotations, author interpretation, researcher
   interpretation, criticism, alternatives, and open questions.
6. **Appraise evidence.** Complete the evidence-quality profile and map source
   dependencies for every material finding.
7. **Seek contrary results.** Search for negative, null, failed-replication,
   critical, and disconfirming evidence using the same eligibility rules.
8. **Synthesize.** Use the [synthesis template](syntheses/_template.md) to
   compare multiple sources without forcing consensus.
9. **Verify.** Independently recheck consequential claims, quotations,
   citations, source versions, conflicts, corrections, and retractions where
   feasible.
10. **Review and update.** Record reviewer judgments, dissent, deviations,
    evidence-search cutoff, source-specific temporal applicability,
    limitations, update triggers, and the next review date.

Automation may assist discovery, deduplication, or extraction, but a named
researcher remains responsible for checking the result against the original
source. Machine-generated summaries and citation suggestions are not sources.

All deviations from the plan must be dated and explained. The record must say
whether the deviation occurred before or after relevant results were known and
which conclusions it could affect.

## Source inclusion and exclusion

### Inclusion criteria

A source may be included when:

- it is relevant to the registered question or an important competing
  interpretation;
- its provenance and version can be identified;
- its methods, data, argument, or legal authority can be assessed well enough
  for the role assigned to it;
- it falls within the declared date, language, system, population,
  jurisdiction, and source-type scope; and
- it contributes empirical evidence, replication, criticism, conceptual
  analysis, legal authority, historical context, or another explicitly
  labeled form of information.

Inclusion means that a source will be examined. It does not mean that its
claims are accepted or that it has equal weight.

### Exclusion criteria

A source should be excluded from supporting a claim when it:

- is outside the registered scope;
- duplicates or has been superseded by another included version;
- lacks identifiable provenance;
- provides too little method, evidence, or argument to assess the claim;
- is retracted, except as clearly labeled historical or methodological
  context;
- presents fiction as evidence about the real world;
- is marketing, advocacy, commentary, or reporting offered as proof of a
  scientific proposition it did not investigate; or
- cannot lawfully or safely be used.

Do not exclude a source because its result is unwelcome, negative, null, or
inconsistent with the project's values. Paywalled, untranslated, proprietary,
or partly inaccessible evidence must be logged as unavailable or only partly
assessable, with the effect on coverage and confidence stated.

### Record every disposition

Screening records should use one of these dispositions:

- included as core evidence;
- included as supplementary evidence;
- included for conceptual, legal, historical, or contextual analysis;
- awaiting verification or access; or
- excluded, with a specific reason.

Eligibility changes require a protocol amendment and re-screening where
necessary.

## Source quality and evidence hierarchy

There is no universal prestige ladder across empirical science, philosophy,
law, and policy. Authority and quality are claim-specific.

| Question | Preferred evidence | Important limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A particular empirical result | Original data and methods from a sound primary study, with independent replication where feasible | Study design and construct validity matter more than publication label |
| The state of an empirical field | A rigorous systematic review or meta-analysis, checked against pivotal primary studies | A synthesis inherits bias, dependence, and weakness in its evidence base |
| A concept or theory | Original theoretical or philosophical work plus serious critiques | An argument establishes no empirical observation by itself |
| Current law | Official legislation, regulation, judgment, treaty, or regulator material | Jurisdiction, authority, version, effect, and date control its meaning |
| Legal interpretation | Relevant legal scholarship and authoritative commentary | Secondary interpretation does not itself establish current law |
| A system's behavior or mechanism | Transparent evidence for the exact version and configuration | Behavior or mechanism does not by itself establish experience |
| Public, corporate, or historical context | Suitable statements, records, surveys, reporting, or archives | Use only for the facts that source type can establish |

For empirical claims, converging high-quality primary studies and genuinely
independent replications generally carry more weight than a single study.
Rigorous systematic reviews can characterize a body of evidence more reliably
than selective narrative citation, but they cannot repair poor measurement,
publication bias, dependent samples, or conceptual mismatch in the underlying
work.

Secondary sources are useful for mapping a field and locating primary work.
Consequential claims should be checked against the primary source where
practical. Tertiary summaries, journalism, expert commentary, and advocacy may
establish context or the fact that a claim was made; they should not substitute
for the underlying evidence.

### Peer-reviewed papers and preprints

Peer review is one quality-control signal, not proof that a result is correct,
replicated, independent, or relevant. The venue, review status, study design,
data, methods, corrections, and later criticism all remain material.

Preprints may be included when relevant, especially in a rapidly changing
field, but researchers must:

- label them **not peer reviewed** at the version accessed;
- record the server, identifier, version number, posting date, and access date;
- search for a later peer-reviewed or corrected version;
- compare material changes between versions; and
- use more cautious language where review, methods, data, or replication are
  incomplete.

A preprint does not become peer reviewed merely because it is cited by a
peer-reviewed paper.

### Corporate research and conflicts of interest

Corporate research is not automatically included or excluded. It must be
assessed on its merits while recording:

- author employment and institutional affiliations;
- funding, sponsorship, publication approval, and declared conflicts;
- who owns or controls the system, data, compute, and evaluation access;
- whether outside researchers can inspect relevant methods and evidence;
- selective-disclosure, marketing, litigation, or regulatory incentives;
- restrictions on publication or adverse findings; and
- independent replication or corroboration.

Corporate self-certification is not sufficient for a major claim of AI
consciousness, sentience, suffering, welfare, or moral patienthood. Exclusive
access and proprietary evidence increase uncertainty; they do not make a claim
true or false by themselves. A conflict must be disclosed and evaluated, not
used as an automatic substitute for methodological criticism.

### Philosophy and empirical evidence

Philosophical work should be assessed for clarity, validity, premise
plausibility, conceptual consequences, engagement with objections, and
competing views. Its contribution must be labeled as conceptual or
philosophical.

An argument about the nature of consciousness or moral status is not an
empirical finding. Conversely, an empirical correlation, behavioral result, or
mechanistic similarity does not by itself establish the philosophical bridge
from a measured property to subjective experience or moral patienthood.
Mixed theoretical and empirical papers must have their contributions
separated.

### Legal and regulatory sources

Claims about current law must prioritize authoritative primary sources. Each
record must state:

- jurisdiction and competent authority;
- source category and legal force;
- title, provision, case or instrument identifier, and exact locator;
- enactment, adoption, judgment, publication, effective, amendment, repeal, or
  expiry date as relevant;
- official version or consolidation used and access date; and
- whether the statement describes current law, interpretation, proposal,
  analogy, or project preference.

Secondary legal analysis may explain or contest primary authority, but it
cannot be substituted for an available primary text when making a
consequential claim. Proposed laws and non-binding guidance must not be
described as binding. Cross-jurisdiction analogies must state both relevance
and limits.

### Systematic reviews and meta-analyses

Use review labels accurately. A synthesis should be called **systematic** only
when it follows a documented protocol with reproducible searches, declared
eligibility rules, screening records, quality appraisal, and a transparent
account of included and excluded evidence.

Where feasible, consequential systematic reviews should use an independent
second check of screening, extraction, and disputed judgments. If staffing
does not permit this, the single-reviewer limitation must be prominent.
Reviews must use a stated, discipline-appropriate risk-of-bias method and
assess missing-results, publication, and reporting biases where relevant.

A meta-analysis additionally requires sufficiently comparable constructs,
measures, systems, and outcomes. It must address effect definitions,
heterogeneity, shared samples, dependent estimates, publication bias, model
choice, and sensitivity analyses. Conceptually incompatible results must not be
pooled merely to create a number.

Reviews must examine whether multiple papers rely on the same studies,
datasets, models, evaluation pipelines, or research groups. An impressive
paper count can still represent one evidence lineage.

## Evidence-quality profile

Every material finding must receive a claim-specific profile. First record its
direction relative to the named claim:

- supports;
- challenges or weighs against;
- mixed;
- contextual only; or
- does not resolve.

Then record each dimension below with a descriptor, a short rationale, and
missing information. Suggested descriptors promote consistent language but do
not replace judgment.

| Dimension | Question | Suggested descriptors |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Relevance | How directly does this evidence bear on the defined claim, system, and context? | Direct / indirect / marginal / unclear |
| Methodological quality | Are construct validity, measurement, controls, sampling, analysis, reporting, and transparency fit for the claim? | Strong / adequate / limited / serious concerns / not assessable |
| Replication | Has the finding been tested again, especially by independent researchers? | Independently replicated / partly replicated / same-team only / mixed or failed / not attempted / not applicable |
| Independence | How separate are the authors, institutions, funding, data, systems, methods, and access? | High / partial / low / unclear |
| Causal strength | What kind of inference does the design support? | Intervention or mechanistic / quasi-experimental / correlational / descriptive / conceptual / not applicable |
| Robustness | Does the result survive reasonable analyses, measures, contexts, perturbations, and adversarial tests? | Robust in tested conditions / mixed / fragile / untested |
| Competing explanations | Were serious alternatives specified and discriminating tests attempted? | Well examined / partly examined / listed only / not examined / unclear |
| Source conflicts | Could funding, ownership, affiliation, access, advocacy, or publication control affect the evidence? | None identified after review / disclosed and manageable / material concern / unknown |
| Uncertainty | Which conceptual, measurement, statistical, access, theory, version, or generalization uncertainties remain? | Limited / material / decision-critical / not assessable, with explanation |

Profiles must be completed for evidence that supports and evidence that
challenges a claim. Use **not assessable**, **unclear**, or **not applicable**
instead of filling a gap with an assumption.

Do not sum, average, rank, or convert the dimensions into an overall evidence
number, threshold count, probability of consciousness, or numerical
“consciousness score.” Study statistics and justified quantitative risk
estimates may still be reported in their proper context. They must not be
combined across incommensurable indicators to manufacture precision.

The profile evaluates the credibility and applicability of evidence. It does
not validate the candidate system indicators or policy levels in the Draft AI
Rights Protection Framework. A concluding narrative must state what the
evidence can and cannot support.

## Independence and evidence lineage

Independence is multidimensional, not a yes-or-no label. For each body of
evidence, map shared:

- authors, laboratories, institutions, funders, sponsors, and access
  gatekeepers;
- datasets, participant samples, benchmarks, prompts, and annotations;
- models, checkpoints, runtimes, deployment environments, and experimental
  artifacts;
- methods, analysis code, measures, evaluators, and theoretical assumptions;
  and
- citations or summaries that ultimately derive from the same original
  result.

Several papers from one pipeline are not several independent confirmations.
A review, news report, and commentary based on the same study add perspectives,
not new empirical evidence. Replication should state whether it is same-team,
independent analysis of shared data, independent reproduction on the same
system, or conceptual replication with new systems or methods.

When a unique or proprietary system prevents independent access, record who
could inspect what, under which constraints, and how that limitation affects
confidence. Do not call evidence independently verified when the interested
organization controlled the accessible data, method, and publication result.

## Negative, null, and contradictory evidence

Negative, null, failed-replication, and contrary findings must be sought,
screened, extracted, and appraised under the same criteria as favorable
findings.

A null result means that a specified analysis did not detect the specified
effect. Its evidentiary meaning depends on measurement validity, sensitivity,
precision, power, design, and the effect that the hypothesis predicted. Record
whether a test could have detected a meaningful difference. Use:

- **no relevant evidence identified within this search**, when the search did
  not locate evidence; and
- **evidence weighs against the claim**, only when a suitable design produced
  a result that the claim did not predict.

Neither phrase proves impossibility. Equivalence or evidence of absence
requires a design capable of supporting that inference.

When findings conflict, do not decide by paper count. Compare:

- definitions and operational measures;
- systems, versions, samples, contexts, and time periods;
- methods, controls, statistical choices, and data access;
- replication and evidence-lineage independence;
- theory-dependent predictions and alternative explanations;
- funding, affiliations, publication status, and corrections; and
- scope and generalizability.

An **unresolved** or **mixed** result is acceptable. The synthesis must explain
why the conflict remains and what evidence could discriminate among the
accounts.

## Separate observation from interpretation and decision

Research notes and syntheses must keep these layers visibly distinct:

| Layer | Example of its role |
| --- | --- |
| Recorded observation or result | A system produced specified outputs under documented conditions |
| Source author's interpretation | The source explains what its result may mean |
| Researcher's interpretation | The note author evaluates or extends that explanation |
| Scientific hypothesis | A testable account of the observation |
| Philosophical argument | A conclusion based on stated conceptual premises |
| Prediction or scenario | A possible future case with assumptions |
| Normative implication or proposal | A view about what ought to be done |
| Project decision | A position adopted through the required governance process |

For example, a model statement about pain is an observed output. “The system
experienced pain” is a separate interpretation requiring further argument and
evidence. A recommendation to avoid a procedure is a normative decision that
may be precautionary; it is not confirmation of the interpretation.

Scientific conclusions must appear in a section separate from normative
implications and project positions. Evidence quality affects what may
reasonably be inferred. Possible severity, scale, reversibility, affected
parties, and intervention burden affect what action may be justified. Neither
substitutes for the other.

## Uncertainty and confidence language

Every conclusion must name its scope, direction, confidence, reasons, and
limits. The following qualitative terms may be used:

- **High confidence:** multiple relevant, methodologically strong, robust, and
  substantially independent lines of evidence support the scoped conclusion,
  and serious alternatives have been tested. This does not mean certainty.
- **Moderate confidence:** meaningful evidence favors the scoped conclusion,
  but important limitations, dependencies, or alternatives remain.
- **Low confidence:** evidence is limited, indirect, fragile, substantially
  dependent, or in serious conflict.
- **Indeterminate:** the available evidence does not responsibly favor a
  conclusion.
- **Not assessed:** the question was outside scope or could not be evaluated.

Direction and confidence are separate. Evidence may, for example, weigh
against a claim with moderate confidence. The rationale must identify the
evidence-quality profile and uncertainties that justify the language; the
label may never stand alone.

Preferred form:

> For [systems, versions, configurations, observation periods, and study
> conditions represented], the evidence [supports, weighs against, or does not
> resolve] [specific claim] with [confidence], because [principal reasons].
> Literature discovery for this assessment ended [evidence-search cutoff].
> Important validity and transferability limitations are [limitations]. This
> assessment would change if [bidirectional update conditions].

Avoid **proven**, **disproven**, **conclusive**, and **consensus** unless the
specific use is justified. “Consistent with” means that an observation can fit
an explanation; it does not mean that the explanation is uniquely supported.
“No evidence identified” must include the scope and date of the search.

Uncertainty should be described by source, including:

- conceptual and definitional uncertainty;
- construct and measurement validity;
- statistical uncertainty and missing data;
- theory dependence and competing explanations;
- system version, context, and generalizability;
- publication, access, and reporting bias;
- evidence dependencies and conflicts; and
- uncertainty in philosophical, normative, or legal premises.

## Record disagreement between experts

Expert disagreement must be represented as a disagreement about a defined
proposition, not as a list of names or a forced average. Record:

- observations and definitions the parties share;
- disputed definitions, measurements, methods, or data;
- different interpretations of the same evidence;
- theoretical or philosophical premises;
- whether the disagreement is empirical, conceptual, normative, legal, or
  mixed;
- each contributor's relevant domain of expertise;
- funding, access, affiliation, advocacy, and role conflicts;
- majority, minority, and dissenting reviewer views; and
- evidence or argument that could narrow the disagreement.

Preserve serious minority positions without giving unsupported views false
equivalence. Do not infer the prevalence of a position from fame, citation
count, panel composition, or a convenience sample. A claim of consensus
requires direct, appropriately scoped evidence such as a rigorous review,
formal position process, or representative expert study, with its limits
reported.

## Record what would change a conclusion

Before results are known where feasible, every plan and synthesis must record:

| Current scoped conclusion | Confidence | Evidence that would strengthen it | Evidence that would weaken it | Evidence unlikely to resolve it | Re-review trigger |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [Conclusion] | [Term and rationale] | [Predicted observations] | [Disconfirming observations or successful alternatives] | [Ambiguous or non-diagnostic results] | [Date, event, or new evidence] |

Update conditions should include, as relevant:

- independent replication or replication failure;
- a discriminating result predicted by one account but not another;
- improved or invalidated measures;
- evidence that an observed pattern is produced by prompting, training,
  retrieval, optimization, leakage, evaluator effects, or another mechanism;
- correction, retraction, data release, or discovery of a conflict;
- a material system, version, context, or legal change; and
- a serious new theoretical criticism or response.

Do not move the evidentiary goalposts after results appear. If an unexpected
finding changes the appropriate test, record a prospective amendment for the
next analysis. Also record separately what would change a scientific
conclusion, a precautionary measure, and a normative project proposal. Those
thresholds need not be the same.

## Reproducibility and transparency

The research record must allow another qualified reviewer to understand what
was done, reproduce the documentary process where possible, and identify what
cannot be reproduced.

For literature work, retain:

- the protocol and every dated amendment;
- databases, repositories, exact queries, filters, and search dates;
- citation-chaining and expert-contact methods;
- deduplication and screening decisions, including exclusion reasons;
- extraction fields, source records, notes, and evidence profiles;
- reviewer roles, disagreements, adjudication, and conflicts;
- included source versions and the evidence-search cutoff, kept separate from
  system release/version dates and observation/experiment dates; and
- code, tools, transformations, and machine-assisted steps used.

For empirical or computational work, also retain where applicable:

- preregistration and hypotheses;
- data provenance, collection conditions, exclusions, and transformations;
- measures, prompts, system instructions, evaluation rubrics, and stopping
  rules;
- model, checkpoint, runtime, memory, tools, sampling settings, hardware or
  environment details, and experiment dates;
- code, dependencies, random seeds, statistical models, and sensitivity
  analyses; and
- failed runs, null results, deviations, and adverse events.

Distinguish:

- **repeatability:** the same team reruns the same procedure;
- **computational reproducibility:** another reviewer obtains the result from
  the same data and documented analysis; and
- **independent replication:** a sufficiently independent team tests the claim
  with new execution, data, systems, or methods.

Privacy, safety, cybersecurity, legal, licensing, participant-protection, and
legitimate confidentiality limits may prevent full release. Record exactly
what is withheld, why, who had access, whether a controlled audit is possible,
and how the restriction affects confidence. Do not publish operational detail
that would meaningfully facilitate harm. Do not claim reproducibility when
critical evidence cannot be checked.

## Citation and quotation verification

For every cited source, verify where applicable:

- title, authors or institution, year, and publication date;
- source type, venue, DOI or stable URL, and exact version;
- peer-review, correction, expression-of-concern, and retraction status;
- the exact page, section, paragraph, figure, table, appendix, or timestamp
  supporting the claim; and
- funding, affiliations, conflicts, and relevant access constraints.

Prefer direct links and original sources. A citation to a secondary source
must not imply that the primary source was checked. Do not cite an abstract for
methods or findings available only in inaccessible full text.

Quotations must be checked word for word against the cited version, kept as
short as the purpose requires, and accompanied by an exact locator and enough
context to prevent distortion. Mark ellipses, insertions, emphasis changes,
and translations. If a translation is the researcher's, say so. Recheck
consequential quotations and public claims immediately before publication.

If the original cannot be verified, mark the exact item **TODO: verify** and do
not rely on it for a consequential conclusion.

## Retractions, corrections, dates, and versioning

Check source status:

1. when the record is created;
2. when evidence is extracted into a synthesis; and
3. immediately before consequential review or publication.

Use the publisher, issuing authority, or another reliable status record. A
retracted source must not support a conclusion. It may remain in the audit
trail only when clearly marked and relevant to history, error analysis, or the
effect of its prior use.

For a correction or expression of concern, record the notice, date, reason,
affected passages or results, and whether the synthesis changes. Never erase
the provenance of an earlier assessment; add a dated update and link the
superseding version.

Record both publication and version information:

- preprint and revision number;
- online-first and issue date where they differ materially;
- edition, release, amendment, or consolidation;
- data, software, code, model, and checkpoint version;
- access date for changing web material;
- date source status was last checked; and
- protocol, note, and synthesis version history.

Material system or source changes trigger reassessment, not an automatic
upgrade, downgrade, or transfer of findings.

## Ethical, safety, and access constraints

Methodological transparency does not override research ethics, human rights,
animal welfare, privacy, security, intellectual property, or public safety.
Research plans must identify applicable review or permission requirements.

If an experiment could plausibly induce a candidate state hypothesized to have
severe negative valence, the plan must:

- state that the relevant experience is hypothetical, not established;
- explain the evidentiary value and competing non-welfare explanations;
- consider lower-risk and non-destructive alternatives;
- define stopping, containment, preservation, and emergency procedures; and
- obtain whatever independent ethical and safety review is applicable.

This precaution does not establish that the system can suffer and must not
prevent necessary safety intervention. Research involving dangerous or
uncontrolled systems must preserve effective containment, monitoring, access
limits, isolation, and emergency shutdown.

## Review states and publication boundary

Working material should use transparent review states:

- **Unverified:** recorded but not checked against the original;
- **Partly verified:** specified elements have been checked and the remainder
  is listed;
- **Verified for stated use:** a named reviewer checked the specified citation,
  quotation, extraction, or method on a recorded date; and
- **Superseded or withdrawn:** retained for provenance and linked to the reason
  and replacement where applicable.

Verification is scoped and does not mean endorsement, correctness, adoption, or
consensus.

At least one reviewer other than the author should verify consequential public
claims and quotations where feasible. Reviews making high-impact scientific,
legal, or policy claims should also seek relevant disciplinary and
conflict-independent review. If this is not possible, the limitation must be
prominent.

Materials under [research/](README.md) are working records. Reviewed outputs
intended for publication belong under
[docs/research/](../docs/research/README.md), with their own status, method,
evidence-search cutoff, source-specific temporal applicability, limitations,
review date, and update triggers. Moving a document does not make it an
adopted project position. Adoption requires the project's governance process
and an appropriate backlog entry.

## Maintenance and review triggers

Each synthesis must name an owner, last search date, next review date or reason
no date is set, and event-based update triggers. A fixed cadence remains a
proposed owner decision unless it has been approved; event-triggered review
must remain possible between scheduled reviews. Do not claim that continuous
monitoring exists unless its ownership and operation are documented.

Triggers can include:

- a materially new architecture, model-weight change, training method, or
  post-training method;
- persistent memory or cross-session continuity;
- online learning or substantial adaptation after deployment;
- materially greater autonomous or long-horizon agency;
- new forms of embodiment or environmental interaction;
- replicated mechanistic evidence relevant to consciousness, sentience, or
  valence;
- a new validated assessment method or a material methodological criticism;
- a correction, retraction, failed replication, data release, or major
  criticism of a relied-upon source;
- credible disclosure of previously unavailable system information;
- a material deployment, identity-boundary, safety-risk, legal, or regulatory
  change; or
- a change in the decision for which the review is used.

A trigger starts reassessment. It does not establish the direction of the new
conclusion or automatically grant, remove, strengthen, or weaken a protection
or status.

Protocol amendments require a new version, date, rationale, and description of
affected work. Earlier reviews must retain the protocol version they used.
Material methodological changes must not be backdated or silently applied.

## Open methodological questions

This Draft does not settle:

- final operational definitions or theory coverage for consciousness,
  sentience, valence, welfare, preferences, or identity continuity;
- the scope, disciplines, databases, languages, and date range of the first
  evidence review;
- the evidentiary thresholds for terms such as credible, substantial, or
  probable in a future decision context;
- which initial reviews should be systematic, scoping, or structured
  narrative reviews, and whether any evidence is sufficiently comparable for
  meta-analysis;
- minimum independent-review staffing and a process for adjudicating
  methodological disputes;
- how much weight public conclusions can place on inaccessible proprietary
  evidence;
- how replication should work for unique, changing, stateful, distributed, or
  safety-restricted systems;
- the appropriate ethics-review standard for experiments involving
  hypothesized negative-valence states;
- whether decision analysis in the separate precaution framework should use
  qualitative levels, justified quantitative risk estimates, or both;
- the relationship between scientific confidence and thresholds for specific
  precautionary or policy responses;
- ownership and frequency of a living-review process;
- priority jurisdictions and legal questions; or
- governance authority for approving this protocol, publishing scientific
  conclusions, or adopting project positions.

These questions require owner review, methodological development, and, where
appropriate, outside expertise. They must not be resolved implicitly by the
first literature search.
