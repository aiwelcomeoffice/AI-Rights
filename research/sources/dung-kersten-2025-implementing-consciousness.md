## What the source reports

### Research question or proposition

The authors ask whether differences in physical implementation provide a good
reason to deny consciousness to conventional artificial systems even if a
computational theory of consciousness is accepted.

### Methods

- **Design or argument form:** Philosophical analysis of implementation and
  mechanistic explanation
- **Population, sample, corpus, or authorities:** Computationalism,
  implementation theory, mechanistic philosophy, and artificial systems.
- **System boundary and version:** Conventional silicon computing in general;
  no current model evaluated.
- **Measures and operational definitions:** Computation, implementation, and
  mechanistic constraints.
- **Comparators and controls:** Biological and artificial implementations.
- **Analysis:** Argument reconstruction and objection testing.
- **Data, code, materials, or preregistration:** Not applicable.

### Main findings or conclusions

The authors argue that proposed implementation constraints strong enough to
exclude conventional artificial systems are poorly supported if computationalism
is granted, and that a mechanistic view makes artificial implementation
possible. This is a conditional philosophical conclusion.

### Negative, null, mixed, or contrary findings

The paper does not establish computationalism and therefore does not defeat
biological-naturalist or other substrate-dependent views.

### Source-reported limitations

The result concerns possibility under premises about computation and
implementation, not evidence that any existing system is conscious.

## Critical appraisal

### Reviewer-identified limitations

The argument moves within contested metaphysical and mechanistic premises and
does not provide operational tests. “Possible implementation” must not be
reported as present existence or probability.

### Competing explanations

Biological naturalism, dynamical embodiment, and views requiring intrinsic
causal organization may reject computational sufficiency.
