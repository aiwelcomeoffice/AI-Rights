## What the source reports

The authors use **AGI** for roughly median-human performance across most
cognitive tasks and **ASI** for general capability exceeding large,
well-coordinated human-expert collectives across virtually all domains. These
are the report's informal working definitions, not an operational test or a
settled project definition. Its Universal AI/AIXI reference is a theoretical,
incomputable limit, not a realized system (§§3–4).

The report maps four potentially parallel pathways: more compute, model and
data scaling; algorithmic shifts; recursive improvement; and multi-agent
coordination (§5, Table 3). Section 5.4 proposes that coordinated groups might
achieve capabilities beyond individual agents through delegation,
specialization, communication and organizational structure. It discusses
centralized and decentralized arrangements. It explicitly leaves the scaling
of collective capability, the value of homogeneous groups, and the steering
of AI groups as open questions (§5.4; §7.1).

The report lists six broad frictions: data supply, economic/natural resources,
limits of the current neural paradigm, increasing research difficulty, an
abstraction barrier, and deliberate slowdown (§5.5, Table 4). It says their
strength and possible counters are unresolved. Recursive improvement could
accelerate development or fizzle out; physical experiments and manufacturing
have latency even for fast digital researchers (§5.3). The authors describe
their pathway judgments as uncertain and their overall map as incomplete.
They say that passing AGI and entering ASI territory within a decade or two
cannot be dismissed; this is a low-confidence possibility, not a measured
forecast or announced milestone (§7, opening; §7.2). They explicitly assume
sufficiently solved safety and alignment for the technological analysis,
while acknowledging this is not established (§7.1, “AI Safety, Alignment,
Sociocultural”).

These are conceptual arguments, scenarios, and research questions. The report
does not present a controlled multi-agent scaling test, a measured rate of
sustained autonomous recursive improvement, or an evaluated AGI/ASI system.
Its citations to other work are source dependencies, not independent
replications of these broad scenarios.

## Critical appraisal for the defined use

**Researcher interpretation:** The report is useful for separating individual
model progress from organizational capability and for designing discriminating
questions. It does not make group-level *performance* equivalent to a
persistent group *agent*: a latter assessment would need evidence of stable
coordination, shared or external state, group decisions, control boundaries,
and persistence across tasks. It also does not resolve whether additional
agents yield superlinear gains or whether coordination costs dominate.

**Serious alternatives and contrary possibilities:** An apparently capable
collective may be orchestrated by humans or a fixed workflow, while its
individual components remain unchanged. Gains may come from parallel task
throughput rather than a new group-level cognitive capacity. Correlated
errors, communication overhead, resource scarcity, weak abstraction formation,
and safety constraints may limit or reverse scaling gains. The report itself
raises several such bottlenecks; this intake did not test their magnitude.

**Evidence profile — claim that multi-agent coordination is a plausible
pathway to broadly superhuman collective capability:** Direction: contextual
support for a hypothesis, not empirical confirmation.

transition rate is assigned from this source.

## Relevance, limits, and update conditions

This source can inform question design in the existing Draft research threads.
It does not change their evidence cutoffs, claims or review states; nor does it
establish AGI/ASI, collective agency or responsibility in a particular system.
Agency and responsibility remain distinct; institutional accountability stays
with the people and organizations that deploy systems. No project policy or
scientific position follows from this intake.

A useful next test would compare agent groups with matched compute, tasks,
tools and human intervention, varying group size and communication structure;
measure both output quality and coordination/persistence properties. Strong,
replicated gains across diverse tasks with documented group-level control
would strengthen the collective-capability hypothesis. Flat or declining
performance under sufficiently sensitive tests, or gains fully explained by
parallel throughput and human orchestration, would weaken the stronger group
agency inference. A correction, later version, reviewed publication, or such a
study should trigger reappraisal.
