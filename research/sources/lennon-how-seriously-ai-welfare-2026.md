## Source claim and method

The abstract and §§3–5 argue that contemporary consciousness science may miss unconceived theories; the bridge from present indicators to phenomenal experience in AI can therefore be weak. Lennon contrasts this with very high confidence in other humans' consciousness from first-person and shared biological grounds. The argument is mostly about LLMs and policy priority. It accepts the significance of consciousness for welfare under its stated premises, and contends that AI-welfare urgency should be constrained by epistemic uncertainty and human interests.

This is a **philosophical assessment**, not a negative/null test of a robot and not evidence that no robot can have welfare. Extrapolating the article's LLM conclusion to an embodied architecture would require a separate analysis of that architecture and its theory-linked evidence. The argument also depends on contestable premises about the consciousness–welfare relation and credences; these are not measured probabilities here.

## Claim-specific appraisal

**Claim assessed:** Current theory-derived AI indicators may underdetermine subjective experience. **Direction:** Supports a serious contrary argument to strong welfare inference. Relevance is indirect for humanoids; reasoning is explicit and peer-reviewed; replication and causal strength are not applicable; robustness is philosophical, not experimental; uncertainty is material. The strongest competing reply is that convergent, causal, system-specific evidence may warrant some rational update without a complete theory. This record does not settle that reply.

**Supports:** an epistemic challenge to a theory-to-experience inference (abstract, §§3–5). **Does not support:** a classification of a humanoid as non-sentient, a universal zero-weight rule for functional indicators, or a fixed policy priority. **TODO: verify:** independent philosophical review and source-status check.
