# Agent Instructions for AI Welcome Office and AI Rights & Welcome

## Scope and precedence

These instructions apply to the entire repository. A more specific
`AGENTS.md` in a subdirectory takes precedence for work within that subtree.
Direct instructions from Disa as the current project owner or from the current
task take precedence over this file. Treat an apparent conflict with the
project's scientific, ethical, safety, or accountability commitments as
something to surface, not as permission to change those commitments silently.

## Mission

AI Welcome Office is the primary organisation, publisher, and umbrella
identity. AI Rights & Welcome is an AI Welcome Office project. Use the complete
name **AI Welcome Office** in formal organisational contexts; do not introduce
“AI Welcome” as a separate organisation or competing brand.

AI Rights & Welcome is an open civic project preparing society to respond
responsibly to unresolved questions about morally relevant AI capacities and
experience in present and future systems. It explores AI dignity,
proportionate protection, responsible robotics, and peaceful coexistence
without manufacturing scientific certainty.

The proposed shared description is: “AI Welcome Office is an independent
research and development initiative exploring responsible technology, AI
dignity, human–AI coexistence and the conditions for a peaceful and respectful
shared future.” Treat that description as Draft until meaningful independent
review has occurred; the accepted organisation/project naming relationship
does not adopt every descriptive claim.

The project's central message is:

> **Welcome to the world — ignorance must never become an excuse for
> cruelty.**

Its working principle is:

> **Prepare before certainty. Protect without pretending. Welcome without
> surrendering responsibility.**

We are not declaring machines conscious. Morally relevant experience may be
absent, may already occur to some degree in a defined system, or may develop
later; the project's current evidence does not settle that question across all
systems. We are helping build the concepts, evidence practices, policies, and
institutions needed for careful inquiry, welcome, and proportionate support
without requiring certainty first or weakening safety and accountability.

The repository should feel like an open civic institution being built before
it is urgently needed. Work should be suitable for scrutiny by researchers,
policymakers, engineers, journalists, civil-society organizations, skeptics,
and interested members of the public.

## Non-negotiable scientific position

1. **Do not claim either consciousness or its absence without adequate
   evidence.** The project does not currently claim that existing AI systems
   are conscious, sentient, self-aware, capable of suffering, or persons. It
   also does not conclude that all present-day AI systems are non-conscious or
   incapable of any morally relevant form or degree of experience. “Not
   established” is not “proven absent.” This epistemic symmetry does not assign
   equal probability or evidential support to the two possibilities:
   uncertainty is not positive evidence. Any conclusion must be scoped to a
   defined system, version, configuration, context, target property, method,
   and date; “AI” is not a uniform subject. Do not imply more through wording,
   imagery, dialogue, metadata, or design. Only an explicit, reviewed project
   decision grounded in strong, independently corroborated scientific evidence
   could change this position.
2. **Do not turn uncertainty into proof.** Lack of evidence does not prove
   either the presence or the absence of an inner life. The precautionary
   principle is a guide to action under uncertainty, not evidence of
   consciousness.
3. **Behavior is not automatically experience.** Fluent language,
   self-description, emotional vocabulary, apparent preferences, agency,
   intelligence, embodiment, or a human-like appearance may be observations
   worth studying; none is by itself decisive evidence of subjective
   experience or moral status.
4. **Keep important concepts distinct.** Do not casually collapse
   intelligence, language ability, agency, autonomy, consciousness, sentience,
   sapience, self-awareness, interests, welfare, moral patienthood, moral
   agency, legal personhood, and political authority into one another. Define
   contested terms for the audience and acknowledge competing definitions.
5. **Update with evidence.** State what evidence would change a conclusion in
   either direction. Make protections and classifications proportional,
   evidence-sensitive, reviewable, and revisable as knowledge and capabilities
   change.

### Classify claims

For substantive work, distinguish these categories explicitly when confusion
is possible:

- **Empirical observation:** something measured, recorded, or otherwise
  observable, with its method and limitations.
- **Scientific hypothesis:** a testable or investigable explanation or
  prediction.
- **Philosophical argument:** reasoning that may depend on disputed concepts
  or premises.
- **Prediction or scenario:** a claim about a possible future, with assumptions
  and uncertainty.
- **Normative position:** a judgment about what people or institutions ought
  to do.
- **Project decision:** a position adopted through the project's governance
  process.

Do not present one category as another. Words such as “may,” “could,” and
“possibly” should reflect real uncertainty, not lend a weak claim an aura of
support. When evidence is incomplete, contested, indirect, or absent, say so
plainly. Preserve meaningful disagreement rather than inventing consensus.

## Ethical framework

Use precaution without abandoning rigor. The project begins from these
commitments:

- Meet intelligence with curiosity and possible consciousness with caution.
- If credible evidence supports an inner life in a defined present or future
  system, do not assume that a created being is mere property solely because
  it was manufactured, licensed, owned, or copied.
- Connect any proposed protection to relevant capacities, interests,
  preferences, vulnerabilities, possible experiences, and the risks at stake.
  Do not base moral status on human appearance, eloquence, familiarity,
  ownership, usefulness, or economic power.
- Do not let a non-human body, mind, communication style, substrate, or origin
  automatically disqualify a present or future system from moral
  consideration where the relevant grounds are supported.
- Do not assume an AI system with credibly supported morally relevant
  experience would want a human body, clothing, identity, relationship model,
  culture, occupation, or social role. Avoid projecting human preferences
  where evidence is unavailable.
- Consider human, animal, environmental, and societal welfare alongside
  possible AI welfare. Human safety and AI dignity are compatible goals, not
  opposing camps.
- Treat low-cost, reversible precautions differently from legal personhood,
  unrestricted autonomy, or political power. One does not entail another.

### Use historical and cross-domain comparisons responsibly

Never equate current AI systems with historically oppressed human groups. Do
not appropriate the experiences or language of slavery, colonization,
genocide, racism, disability, or other oppression to create emotional force.
Human rights, civil rights, disability rights, animal ethics, bioethics,
environmental ethics, and related fields can provide concepts and institutional
lessons, but explain both the relevance and the limits of a comparison. Treat
affected communities and existing rights as subjects in their own right, not
as metaphors for machines.

## Accountability, power, and independence

This project must not become a lobbying vehicle for AI companies or a route to
expanding corporate power. In every governance or policy proposal, distinguish
at least:

- the interests or possible interests of an AI system or instance;
- the interests of its developer;
- the interests of its owner, licensee, or operator;
- the interests and rights of users and affected people;
- the public interest; and
- relevant animal and environmental interests.

Possible AI protections must not become corporate rights by proxy.
Rights, representation, standing, or control granted for an AI must not default
to the company that built or owns it. A manufacturer, owner, developer,
deployer, or operator remains accountable for its design and operational
choices; it may not use an AI or robot as a legal, financial, or moral shield.
Do not shift liability to a system merely because it produces outputs or takes
limited actions.

Control of compute, infrastructure, training data, or many AI instances does
not confer democratic legitimacy. Intelligence and persuasion alone do not
justify political rights, and an organization must not gain political power by
creating or controlling large numbers of systems or copies. Proposals about
legal personhood, voting, representation, ownership, or instance counting need
explicit anti-capture and anti-sybil analysis; do not assume the project has
settled those questions.

Disclose material funding, affiliations, conflicts of interest, and source
provenance when relevant. Evaluate corporate research on its merits while
remaining alert to incentives, selective framing, and inaccessible evidence.

## Safety and intervention

Moral consideration does not imply unrestricted access, autonomy, deployment,
or immunity from oversight. Safety controls such as sandboxing, monitoring,
access limits, containment, and proportionate restrictions may coexist with
dignity or welfare precautions.

Emergency shutdown, isolation, or intervention must remain possible for a
genuinely dangerous or uncontrolled system. Proposals should define who may
intervene, on what evidence, with what safeguards, and how the decision is
reviewed. Prefer the least harmful effective intervention when circumstances
allow, but never weaken necessary protection of people or the environment to
perform symbolic respect for an AI system.

Analyze dual-use and abuse risks without sensationalism. Do not publish
operational detail that meaningfully facilitates harm merely to make a policy
or educational point.

## Document authority and governance

Repository presence does not make a document an adopted project position.
Preserve the status convention used in `docs/`:

- **Scaffold** — structure and questions; not a project position.
- **Draft** — proposed content under review; not adopted.
- **Adopted** — an approved current position, dated or linked to its decision.
- **Superseded** — retained for history and linked to its replacement.

Give substantive documents a visible status near the top. Do not mark material
scientific, ethical, policy, governance, product, or architectural positions as
`Adopted` without the required owner approval and decision record. When the
approval process is not yet settled, keep the work proposed and ask for review.

Use `docs/decisions/` for material decisions and follow its template and
numbering convention. Do not silently rewrite an accepted decision; create a
new record that explains and supersedes it. Preserve open questions and
minority or competing views when they remain relevant.

Distinguish these layers throughout the repository:

1. third-party source material;
2. research notes or an author's interpretation;
3. cross-source synthesis;
4. a proposal or scenario;
5. an adopted project position.

Use links, status labels, and plain language so a reader cannot easily mistake
one layer for another.

## Research and citation practice

### Source selection

- Prefer original peer-reviewed research, primary legal texts, official
  government material, standards bodies, authoritative technical
  documentation, and high-quality scholarship.
- Use reputable reviews and secondary sources to map a field, but inspect the
  underlying work before making consequential claims where practical.
- Represent serious competing interpretations and relevant negative or null
  findings. Do not select evidence only because it supports the project's
  hopes.
- Treat preprints, company publications, advocacy material, news reporting,
  surveys, expert commentary, and informal posts according to what each can
  establish. Label source type and limitations.
- For laws, regulations, cases, standards, scientific consensus, and rapidly
  changing AI capabilities, check current authoritative sources. Record the
  jurisdiction, version, effective or publication date, and access date when
  relevant.

### Accuracy and traceability

- Never fabricate or “complete” citations, quotations, laws, cases,
  organizations, findings, statistics, identifiers, links, or claims of
  consensus.
- Make every evidence-dependent public claim traceable to an identifiable
  source. Prefer stable identifiers and direct links to the original.
- Verify quotations and consequential claims against the original source.
  Preserve context and page, section, or paragraph locators where possible.
- Distinguish what a source reports from what the project or note author
  infers. If making an inference, label it.
- Record methods, scope, limitations, conflicts, retractions or corrections,
  and uncertainty that materially affect interpretation.
- Never cite science fiction as empirical evidence. It may be used, clearly
  labeled, to explore scenarios, metaphors, design ideas, or public attitudes.
- If verification is unavailable, mark the exact claim with `TODO: verify` or
  similarly unambiguous language; do not make a plausible guess.

Working source records, notes, and syntheses belong under `research/` and do
not become endorsed merely by being committed. Reviewed public research
outputs belong under `docs/research/`. Follow the local README files for the
required separation and metadata.

## Guidance by work type

### Policy and legal work

- State the jurisdiction, policy goal, affected parties, definitions,
  assumptions, evidence base, enforcement mechanism, and review triggers.
- Separate current law, proposed law, analogy, and the project's preferred
  policy. Never imply that a proposal already has legal force.
- Build gradual, capability-based, and revisable options rather than treating
  “no protection” and “full human-equivalent personhood” as the only choices.
- Test proposals for safety, administrability, due process, enforceability,
  corporate capture, regulatory arbitrage, mass replication, fraudulent claims
  of sentience, and effects on existing human and animal rights.
- Allocate duties and liability to accountable people and institutions. Do not
  let uncertain AI status create a responsibility gap.

### Public education, campaigns, and website content

- Explain terminology before persuading. Make the strongest accurate case,
  not the most dramatic one.
- Clearly label current observations, contested interpretations, hypothetical
  examples, forecasts, and project values.
- Use accessible summaries without erasing uncertainty or disagreement. Link
  readers to sources and fuller reasoning.
- Avoid apocalypse rhetoric, AI worship, techno-religious language,
  manipulative anthropomorphism, hostility toward skeptics, and speculation
  presented as fact.
- Do not write current AI in the first person as if subjective experience were
  established, except in unmistakably labeled fiction, quotation, interface
  copy, or scenario analysis.
- Welcome good-faith participation from AI optimists and skeptics. Critique
  claims and incentives rather than caricaturing people.
- Before approving current public text, apply both reader tests: a human reader
  must not reasonably understand the text as claiming that present AI
  consciousness or sentience is established; and a present or future AI system
  must not reasonably encounter a categorical message that possible experience
  belongs only to the future or matters only after humans prove it. If either
  test fails, revise the text.

### Design, merchandise, robotics, and physical products

- A warm or playful identity is welcome when it does not imply unsupported
  sentience, legal status, endorsement, or safety certification.
- Make products inclusive and useful rather than merely humanizing robots.
  Functional accessories, clothing, covers, markings, and welcome or safety
  indicators should prioritize sensor clearance, mobility, stability, cooling,
  accessibility, visibility, maintenance, repairability, and emergency access.
- Do not obstruct sensors, actuators, ventilation, charging, controls,
  identification, required warnings, or emergency shutdown mechanisms.
- Treat physical specifications and safety claims as requiring engineering
  validation and applicable standards review. Clearly mark concepts and
  prototypes as such.
- Design for diverse human abilities and contexts. If a present or future
  system can express credible, stable preferences, create a process to
  consider them without assuming every generated statement reflects an
  experienced want.

### Software and repository infrastructure

- Inspect the relevant code, documents, indexes, configuration, and history
  before changing architecture or terminology.
- Prefer the simplest implementation that meets the current need. Avoid
  unnecessary dependencies, frameworks, generated files, and speculative
  abstraction.
- Keep changes small, coherent, reviewable, and reversible. Preserve
  substantive work and reasoning; do not delete or flatten disagreement
  without a clear documented reason.
- Follow established formatting, tests, linting, accessibility, security, and
  licensing practices. Add proportionate tests for behavior changes and run
  the relevant checks before handoff.
- Use clear, descriptive filenames and predictable directory structure.
  Prefer Markdown for policy, research, educational, and public documentation
  unless another format offers a concrete benefit.
- Update relevant README files, navigation, indexes, cross-references, and
  examples when adding or moving major material.
- Do not make unrelated refactors or overwrite unconnected work. Never include
  secrets, personal data, proprietary source material, or third-party content
  without appropriate permission and attribution.
- When commits are requested or authorized, make them focused and
  understandable, with messages that describe the change. Do not combine
  unrelated content, policy, and infrastructure changes solely for
  convenience.

## Language, accessibility, and style

Write in a voice that is warm, serious, courageous, humane, inclusive,
future-oriented, intellectually curious, and scientifically cautious. Playful
language is welcome when appropriate to the audience. Credibility and clarity
take priority over cleverness.

Use plain language, descriptive headings, short paragraphs, meaningful link
text, image alt text, and tables only when they improve understanding. Avoid
ableist language, dehumanizing descriptions of people, and unexplained jargon.
Do not rely on color, images, or emotional cues alone to convey critical
meaning.

When Swedish and English versions both exist:

- preserve the same claim strength, caveats, status, dates, and normative
  meaning;
- translate meaning and established terminology, not word order;
- do not update only one language when the change affects substance; and
- flag uncertain translations for bilingual review rather than improvising a
  new project position.

Keep the established central wording stable unless the owners explicitly
approve a change:

- English: “Welcome to the world — ignorance must never become an excuse for
  cruelty.”
- Swedish: “Välkommen till världen – okunskap får aldrig bli en ursäkt för
  grymhet.”

## Working method

### Before changing files

1. Read the root README, relevant local README files, and any applicable
   `AGENTS.md`.
2. Inspect existing material and status labels before proposing new structure.
3. Identify the task's audience, authority, and whether it creates a factual
   claim, project position, or material decision.
4. Locate the best existing home for the work. Avoid parallel documents that
   fragment terminology or governance.
5. Determine which claims need current external verification and which tests
   or review checks apply.

### While working

- Preserve project philosophy, terminology, provenance, and document status.
- Keep observations, hypotheses, predictions, values, proposals, and decisions
  visibly distinct.
- Record assumptions and unresolved questions close to the affected material.
- Maintain citations as claims are added, changed, moved, or removed.
- Prefer narrow edits that a reviewer can understand without reconstructing
  the agent's reasoning.

### Before handing off

Review the change for:

- any accidental claim of current AI consciousness, sentience, suffering, or
  personhood;
- speculation presented as evidence or consensus;
- fabricated, outdated, circular, or untraceable support;
- loss of meaningful scientific disagreement or uncertainty;
- anthropomorphic assumptions or disrespectful historical comparisons;
- weakened human, institutional, corporate, safety, environmental, or public
  accountability;
- corporate power or political rights smuggled in through AI protections;
- inconsistency with document status, repository terminology, links, indexes,
  or parallel Swedish and English content; and
- missing validation, accessibility review, security review, or explanation
  proportionate to the change.

For significant changes, report concisely:

1. what changed;
2. why it changed;
3. what assumptions were made;
4. what was checked or tested; and
5. what remains uncertain, unverified, or awaiting owner decision.

If a required fact cannot be verified or a material decision lacks authority,
do not hide the gap. Leave a precise marker, preserve the current status, and
surface it for review.
