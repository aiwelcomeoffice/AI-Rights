## What the source reports

### Research question or proposition

Wiese asks what the free energy principle implies for artificial consciousness
and whether computational simulation is sufficient.

### Methods

- **Design or argument form:** Philosophical analysis of a scientific framework
- **Population, sample, corpus, or authorities:** Free energy principle,
  computational functionalism, active inference, and consciousness literature.
- **System boundary and version:** Artificial systems in principle, not a
  specific model.
- **Measures and operational definitions:** Organizational, dynamical, and
  representational constraints associated with active inference.
- **Comparators and controls:** Weak computational functionalism versus stronger
  mechanistic constraints.
- **Analysis:** Conditional theoretical reasoning.
- **Data, code, materials, or preregistration:** Not applicable.

### Main findings or conclusions

The paper challenges an automatic inference from computational simulation to
consciousness and argues that free-energy-based accounts may impose substantive
organizational and dynamical requirements. This is an argument within a
contested framework, not an empirical disproof.

### Negative, null, mixed, or contrary findings

The analysis leaves artificial consciousness open if the relevant constraints
can be realized; it does not make biological substrate alone necessary.

### Source-reported limitations

Implications depend on how the free energy principle and relevant mechanistic
notions are interpreted.

## Critical appraisal

### Reviewer-identified limitations

The free energy principle's breadth creates a risk that generic system behavior
is redescribed rather than discriminatively predicted. No validated AI
indicator or consciousness outcome is supplied.

### Competing explanations

Weak functionalism treats the right computation as sufficient; biological
naturalism requires life; deflationary views may deny that the proposed
features identify phenomenal experience.
