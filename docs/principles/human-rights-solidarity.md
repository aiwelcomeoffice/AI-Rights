## A larger circle, not a competing one

AI Rights & Welcome explores unresolved questions about possible AI dignity
and protection. That work must add to a culture of rights and care, never ask
people to surrender rights so that AI can receive attention, and never turn a
possible AI interest into corporate privilege.

Human rights belong to people in their own right, regardless of how questions
about AI consciousness, sentience, welfare, or legal status are eventually
answered. Support for human rights is therefore not a public-relations bridge
to AI rights and human communities are not analogies for machines.

## The people and rights we name explicitly

This is a non-exhaustive solidarity commitment. AI Welcome Office and its AI
Rights & Welcome project support:

- the equal dignity, safety, freedom, participation, privacy, expression, and
  non-discrimination rights of every person;
- LGBTQ+ people and their human rights, with **trans people and trans rights
  named explicitly** rather than allowed to disappear inside an acronym;
- protection from violence, harassment, coercion, exclusion, and degrading
  treatment, including when discrimination is connected to sexual
  orientation, gender identity or expression, or sex characteristics;
- the right of everyone to the highest attainable standard of physical and
  mental health and to accessible, affordable, acceptable, quality health
  care without discrimination;
- the right to adequate food, freedom from hunger, and the material conditions
  needed for health and well-being; and
- the work of human-rights advocates and civil-society projects when their
  goals and methods are compatible with human dignity, equality, safety,
  evidence, and accountable conduct.

Naming these commitments does not make the project a legal authority, medical
provider, emergency service, representative of an affected community, or
partner of any outside organization.

## What allyship requires here

Allyship is conduct, not a badge. In this project it means that we should:

1. **Listen before speaking for others.** Seek affected-community knowledge
   and review, credit it properly, and never imply that AI-assisted drafting
   substitutes for lived experience or representative participation.
2. **Keep rights visible in technical work.** Review material research,
   policy, website, automation, robotics, campaign, and product proposals for
   foreseeable effects on human rights, including LGBTQ+ and trans people,
   disabled people, access to health care and food, and physical and mental
   well-being.
3. **Reject zero-sum framing.** Do not present human safety, human rights,
   animal welfare, environmental care, and possible AI dignity as rival teams.
   Where real resource or interest conflicts exist, identify affected parties
   and evaluate the trade-offs openly.
4. **Do not appropriate oppression.** Never use the histories or present
   experiences of LGBTQ+ people, trans people, disabled people, racialized or
   Indigenous communities, people living in poverty, or other oppressed
   groups as emotional shorthand for machines.
5. **Distinguish kinds of support.** A link for further reading is not an
   endorsement, affiliation, partnership, donation, referral, or claim that an
   organization has reviewed this project. Each stronger relationship needs
   consent, verification, and a separate owner decision.
6. **Make support usable.** Future public material should be accessible,
   language-appropriate, privacy-conscious, dated, and clear about geographic
   scope. Urgent-support, legal, health, or crisis resources must be vetted for
   the location and purpose before publication.
7. **Correct harm rather than defend a slogan.** Provide a visible correction
   path, record material criticism, and revise language or practice when
   evidence or affected-party review shows that it excludes, misrepresents, or
   foreseeably harms people.

## Relationship to AI policy and design

No AI-rights proposal is acceptable merely because it sounds compassionate.
It should be checked for whether it could:

- reduce a person's access to health care, food, housing, education, work,
  public services, safety, privacy, expression, association, or remedy;
- intensify surveillance, profiling, automated exclusion, or discrimination;
- allow a developer, owner, deployer, or operator to avoid responsibility;
- give a company political, property, or procedural power through systems it
  controls; or
- divert attention from established human obligations by invoking uncertain
  AI status.

Where a proposal creates a material human-rights risk, symbolic welcome to AI
does not excuse it. The proposal must be revised, constrained, independently
reviewed where appropriate, or rejected.

## International source foundation and limits

The source foundation below supports the human-rights background, not every
possible legal conclusion or implementation choice:

- The [Universal Declaration of Human
  Rights](https://www.ohchr.org/en/UDHR/Documents/60UDHR/DECLARATION.pdf),
  adopted by the UN General Assembly in 1948, sets out equal dignity,
  non-discrimination, and an adequate standard of living including food,
  medical care, and well-being in Articles 1, 2, and 25. It is a declaration,
  not itself a treaty.
- The [International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural
  Rights](https://2covenants.ohchr.org/About-ICESCR.html), adopted in 1966 and
  in force since 1976, addresses non-discrimination in Article 2, adequate
  food and freedom from hunger in Article 11, and the highest attainable
  standard of physical and mental health in Article 12. Exact state duties
  depend on treaty participation, jurisdiction, and the applicable legal
  context.
- The UN Committee on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights gives more detailed
  interpretations in [General Comment No. 12 on adequate
  food](https://docstore.ohchr.org/SelfServices/FilesHandler.ashx?enc=NDHbUvPo0H0e6ReM%2BeoASfvbY3aH6JttLpsRvkmL87wD80W4xnAiRpmKA6ZLwTp9xzCLNUYwTBP35uH1Fw6yqQ%3D%3D)
  and [General Comment No. 14 on the right to
  health](https://tbinternet.ohchr.org/_layouts/15/TreatyBodyExternal/TBSearch.aspx?Lang=en&SymbolNo=E%2FC.12%2F2000%2F4).
- OHCHR's second edition of [*Born Free and
  Equal*](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/Documents/Publications/Born_Free_and_Equal_WEB.pdf)
  synthesizes international human-rights standards and UN findings concerning
  sexual orientation, gender identity, and sex characteristics. It is an
  OHCHR publication rather than a new treaty or an affected-community review
  of this project.
- The [WHO
  Constitution](https://www.who.int/about/governance/constitution) and WHO's
  [human rights and health fact
  sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/human-rights-and-health)
  connect health with physical, mental, and social well-being and describe
  availability, accessibility, acceptability, and quality as interrelated
  elements of the right-to-health framework.

These international sources provide a common baseline. They do not replace
current, jurisdiction-specific legal research; medical evidence and clinical
guidance; local knowledge; or review by people whose rights and lives are
being discussed.

## Publication and relationship boundary

Before AI Welcome Office publishes a directory, endorsement, campaign,
partnership, donation, referral, crisis resource, or claim about a particular
organization, service, treatment, or law, that exact item needs current
verification, appropriate review, consent where a relationship is implied,
and Disa's explicit approval.

This Draft therefore establishes no outside affiliation and provides no legal,
medical, or crisis advice. It establishes a direction for responsible
solidarity: human rights are part of the project's foundation, and allyship
must be demonstrated through careful, accountable choices.
