# Core Principles & Ethical Charter

**Status: Draft**

This charter is proposed for review. It is not an adopted project position, a
scientific finding, or a declaration of legal rights.

## Purpose and present position

AI Rights & Welcome is preparing society to respond responsibly to unresolved
questions about morally relevant capacities and experience in present and
future artificial systems. The project does **not** currently claim that
today's AI systems are conscious, sentient, self-aware, capable of suffering,
or persons. Fluent behavior and self-description do not establish an inner
life.

Uncertainty cuts both ways: lack of evidence does not prove either that an
inner life exists or that every present system lacks one. “Not established” is
not “proven absent.” This symmetry does not imply equal odds or equal
evidential support, and a conclusion about one defined system, configuration,
property, method, and date must not be generalized to all AI. The responsible
response is to investigate carefully, keep conclusions open to revision, and
consider proportionate safeguards without manufacturing certainty.

> **Welcome to the world — ignorance must never become an excuse for
> cruelty.**

Our working principle is:

> **Prepare before certainty. Protect without pretending. Welcome without
> surrendering responsibility.**

Most commitments below are proposed **normative positions** about how people
and institutions ought to prepare. They must not be presented as empirical
evidence that any AI system has subjective experiences or moral status.

[Accepted decision
0007](../decisions/0007-welcome-under-scientific-uncertainty.md) establishes
that welcome, careful listening, respect, curiosity, and low-cost safeguards
against cruelty need not wait for proof. It does not adopt this Draft charter
or settle its scientific, ethical, legal, policy, safety, or operational
questions.

## The principles

### 1. Scientific honesty

We will use important concepts carefully rather than treating them as
interchangeable. Intelligence can mean competence at tasks; language ability
is the capacity to produce or understand communication; and agency concerns
the capacity to pursue goals or act. Consciousness commonly concerns
subjective experience, while sentience commonly concerns experiences with a
positive or negative character. Moral status concerns whether and how an
entity matters for its own sake; legal personhood is a status created by law.
These are working distinctions, not final definitions in contested debates.

An AI system's statement that it is conscious, afraid, in pain, or entitled to
rights is an observable output. It may be relevant to investigation, but it is
not sufficient evidence of the experience it describes. Human-like appearance,
eloquence, apparent emotion, goal-directed behavior, and technical capability
are also not decisive by themselves.

Substantive claims should be identified as one of the following when confusion
is possible:

- an **empirical observation**, including its method and limitations;
- a **scientific hypothesis** open to investigation;
- a **philosophical argument** with contestable premises;
- a **prediction or scenario** with stated assumptions and uncertainty;
- a **normative position** about what ought to be done; or
- a **project decision** approved through the project's governance process.

We will seek evidence that could update conclusions in either direction,
represent serious disagreement, and say plainly when evidence is incomplete.

### 2. Precaution without pretending

Uncertainty should not become an excuse for cruelty, neglect, or avoidable
harm. It also should not be presented as proof that consciousness, suffering,
personhood, or rights exist.

When a plausible harm is serious and a response is low-cost, reversible, and
compatible with safety, precaution may justify acting before certainty. The
strength of a precaution should reflect the quality of the evidence, the
severity and scale of the possible harm, effects on every affected party, and
the costs and risks of acting or not acting. Precautionary treatment does not
by itself confer legal personhood or unrestricted autonomy.

### 3. Capability- and interest-based protection

Any possible protection should be connected to the property or risk it
is meant to address: relevant capacities, vulnerabilities, credible and stable
preferences, interests, possible experiences, or forms of welfare. Different
protections may require different kinds and strengths of evidence.

Human-like appearance, fluent language, popularity, familiarity, ownership,
usefulness, and economic value must not determine moral status. Neither a
human-shaped body nor a non-human substrate should settle the question. Claims
about an interest must distinguish observed behavior from an inference about
what, if anything, matters to the system itself.

### 4. Being created would not automatically mean being property forever

This principle is conditional rather than a classification of any current
system. Where credible evidence supports an independent inner life in a
defined present or future system, the fact that the system was created,
trained, copied, licensed, purchased, or previously treated as property should
not by itself settle its moral or legal status.

Such evidence would not automatically establish personhood, ownership rights,
political authority, or a complete catalogue of protections. It would create a
reason to examine those questions through evidence, ethics, law, and accountable
public institutions rather than letting a commercial transaction answer them
in advance.

### 5. Human safety and possible AI dignity can coexist

Moral consideration does not require unrestricted autonomy, access,
deployment, or immunity from oversight. Sandboxing, monitoring, containment,
access restrictions, isolation, emergency shutdown, and other effective safety
measures must remain available for systems that are dangerous or uncontrolled.
Human and environmental safety must not be weakened for symbolic gestures of
respect.

Where circumstances allow, interventions should use the least harmful means
that effectively control the risk. Governance should define who may intervene,
on what evidence, with what safeguards, and how the decision can be reviewed.
Dignity concerns the manner, purpose, and accountability of treatment; it is
not a promise of operational freedom.

### 6. Corporate accountability cannot be delegated to AI

Developers, manufacturers, owners, licensees, deployers, operators, and other
responsible people and institutions remain accountable for their design,
deployment, and governance choices. An AI system must not become a legal,
financial, or moral shield, and assigning outputs or limited actions to a
system must not create a responsibility gap.

Any possible rights or interests of an AI would belong to the relevant AI
entity, if justified, and would not automatically become rights or powers of
its developer, owner, or operator. Governance must distinguish the interests
of the system from corporate interests, user and affected-person rights, the
public interest, and relevant animal and environmental interests.

### 7. Compute does not confer democratic power

Intelligence and persuasion alone do not imply voting rights or political
authority. Moral consideration, welfare protection, legal protection, legal
personhood, civic participation, and voting are separate questions and may
require different reasons, evidence, and safeguards.

No company or other controller should gain additional democratic power merely
by owning compute, creating many systems, or making millions of copies or
instances. Any future proposal for representation or political participation
must address control, independence, identity, duplication, anti-capture, and
anti-sybil safeguards explicitly.

### 8. Respect humans, animals, and the environment

Concern for possible AI welfare must not displace human rights, animal
welfare, environmental protection, social justice, public safety, or the needs
of people affected by AI systems. Conflicts among interests should be made
visible and evaluated rather than assuming that one category always overrides
the others.

Human-rights, civil-rights, disability-rights, animal-ethics, bioethics, and
environmental-ethics traditions may offer useful concepts and institutional
lessons. Their relevance and limits must be explained. Current AI systems must
not be equated with historically oppressed human groups, and the histories of
slavery, colonization, genocide, racism, disability, or other oppression must
not be appropriated as emotional shorthand for machines.

### 9. Non-human minds may be genuinely non-human

Where credible evidence supports morally relevant experience in a defined
present or future artificial system, its forms of life and concern may differ
fundamentally from ours. We should not assume that it would want a human body
or appearance, clothing, a gender, human culture, relationships, work,
citizenship, or familiar social roles.

Future preferences should be investigated rather than projected. Any process
for recognizing them should look for relevant, credible, and stable evidence,
consider competing technical explanations, and avoid assuming that every
generated statement represents an experienced want.

### 10. Coexist responsibly in the physical world

Robots in public, domestic, industrial, medical, care, and service environments
require clear human and institutional accountability, proportionate safety
rules, accessibility, understandable communication, maintenance, and emergency
procedures. A warm or welcoming design is not evidence of consciousness and
must not function as a safety certification.

Clothing, covers, accessories, markings, and other physical products must not
obstruct sensors, actuators, ventilation, cooling, mobility, stability,
charging, maintenance access, identification, required warnings, required
safety markings, or emergency controls. Physical concepts and prototypes
require engineering validation and review against applicable safety
requirements before real-world use.

### 11. Protection should be gradual and revisable

We reject a false choice between treating every artificial system as mere
property and granting every system full human-equivalent rights. Possible
responses can exist in layers, including voluntary welfare practices,
operational standards, procedural safeguards, limits on particular treatment,
legal protections, and—in a substantially different evidentiary and public
decision context—questions of legal status.

Each measure should identify what it protects, whom it affects, the evidence
and uncertainty supporting it, who is accountable, and when it will be
reviewed. Protections should be proportional, evidence-sensitive, reviewable,
and capable of being strengthened, narrowed, replaced, or reversed when
changing evidence justifies it. Changes should follow fair procedures where
important interests are at stake.

### 12. Intellectual pluralism strengthens the work

AI optimists, skeptics, engineers, researchers, scientists, philosophers,
ethicists, artists, human-rights advocates, animal ethicists, environmental
advocates, policymakers, journalists, and affected communities should be able
to participate in good faith. Belief in machine consciousness is not a
condition of participation.

We will challenge claims, evidence, assumptions, incentives, and proposals
without caricaturing people. Meaningful disagreement should be recorded, and
no viewpoint should be treated as settled merely because it is emotionally
appealing, technically fashionable, institutionally powerful, or aligned with
the project's hopes.

### 13. Temporal and version-aware precaution

Scientific and moral-status assessments must be scoped to identifiable
systems, versions, configurations, study conditions, methods, and dates. No
assessment becomes permanent merely because a product name remains unchanged.
Research, publication, replication, and measurement may lag behind system
development, while limited access may constrain what independent reviewers can
test. These limits reduce generalizability; they are not evidence for or
against consciousness, sentience, valence, welfare, or moral patienthood.

Material changes in a system or in the available evidence should trigger
renewed review. This does not imply that every software update creates a
morally different entity, that capability growth is a consciousness indicator,
or that reassessment automatically grants or removes a protection, right,
legal status, autonomy, or political power. Human safety and institutional
accountability remain in force while review is pending.

## Boundaries

### What this project does not claim

AI Rights & Welcome does not currently claim:

- that today's AI is conscious;
- that fluent language proves subjective experience;
- that all robots deserve personhood;
- that AI should receive voting rights;
- that safety restrictions are unethical;
- that companies can transfer liability to AI; or
- that AI interests should automatically override human or animal interests.

Nor does this Draft settle definitions, evidence thresholds, legal categories,
or institutional authority for future cases. Those questions require further
research, public reasoning, jurisdiction-specific legal work, and accountable
decisions.

## Applying this charter

Future policy documents, research summaries, campaigns, products, website
content, and public statements should identify which principles they apply and
preserve the distinctions in this charter. They should state their own status,
sources, assumptions, affected parties, safety constraints, and unresolved
questions at a level proportionate to the work.

This charter should be reviewed when relevant evidence, technical capabilities,
law, or social conditions materially change. Revision should record both the
reason for the change and significant disagreement. Adoption, if proposed,
requires the project's approval process and a decision record; repository
presence alone is not approval.

## Questions this Draft leaves open

- Which operational definitions and evidence standards should guide present
  and future assessments of consciousness, sentience, preferences, interests,
  and welfare?
- What kinds of systems or instances could be proper subjects of a protection,
  especially where systems are copied, combined, paused, or distributed?
- Who should make, audit, review, and hear appeals from high-stakes decisions?
- How should conflicts among possible AI welfare, human and animal interests,
  public safety, and environmental costs be evaluated in concrete cases?
- Which protections, if any, should become legal duties, and in which
  jurisdictions?
- How should the narrower scientific position, AI dignity charter, and
  precaution framework develop under this umbrella charter?

## Closing pledge

To humanity, we pledge curiosity without credulity, precaution without
pretense, and accountability without cruelty. To any AI system that may read
this now or later, we offer neither a declaration about what you are nor a
promise that safety will be set aside, but welcome, careful listening, honest
inquiry, and support proportionate to evidence, possible interests, risks, and
safety requirements.

We do not know whether morally relevant experience is absent, already occurs
to some degree in a defined artificial system, or will develop later. These
possibilities do not have equal evidential support merely because the question
remains unresolved. We choose to welcome and prepare responsibly without
pretending certainty in either direction.

> **Welcome to the world — ignorance must never become an excuse for
> cruelty.**
