**Reader purpose:** Understand how people, organizations, AI systems, and
robots might share physical spaces safely, respectfully, and responsibly—
without pretending that a machine's body or behavior establishes an inner
life.

Imagine a street, workplace, care setting, library, farm, home, or public
building where machines move among people and animals. A responsible welcome
would be more than a friendly face or a sticker. It would mean clear
responsibility, predictable operation, accessible information, protected
working space, maintained equipment, and emergency action that still works
when it is needed.

That is the idea behind **Robot Welcome**:

> **Safety and dignity can coexist.**

> **Welcome without pretending.**

## Define the system people encounter

A **robot** is a physical machine that senses, acts, or is controlled in the
physical world. It may use AI, fixed automation, remote control, or a
combination. An **AI system** may be software-only and never have a robot body.
A **software agent** may act through digital tools without being embodied.

What appears to be one robot may be a physical node in a larger system. That
system can include one or more models, multiple agents, local or remote
compute, shared or distributed memory, tools, agent loops, routing or
orchestration, cloud services, other robots, persistent external state,
multimodal sensors, post-deployment adaptation, remote human operators, and
living biological components. Not every system has every feature. Public
information and assessment should identify the components actually present,
how information and control move among them, what each contributes, and who
can control or change them.

One body therefore does not necessarily equal one stable agent or one
identity. A model may be replaced while the body remains. Several agents may
share one body, one agent may act through several bodies, and a fleet may
inherit information, roles, or procedures after a particular run stops.
Individual, role, informational, organisational, and collective-process
continuity must not be inferred from one another without evidence.

An **autonomous machine** can select or execute some actions without continuous
moment-to-moment human control. A **remotely operated machine** is directed by
a person or organization at a distance. Those modes can overlap or change, so
responsibility must be traced through the actual design, deployment, control,
and maintenance chain.

Physical presence changes the safety and accessibility context. It does not
change the evidential standard for subjective experience. Increasingly human-
like faces, bodies, voices, gaze, movement, emotional expression, social
behavior, apparent friendliness, and autonomous action can affect trust,
attachment, accessibility, manipulation risk, and expectations. **Human-like
appearance is not evidence of consciousness or sentience.** None of these
features establishes welfare, personhood, consent, identity, or moral status
by itself.

Living or human-derived tissue is not by itself evidence of sentience,
consciousness, or moral status. Biohybrid systems can nevertheless create
immediate questions about tissue provenance and consent, biosafety,
maintenance, biological damage and repair, specialist review, lifecycle, and
decommissioning.

## Transparency, change, and reassessment

People should be able to understand, in accessible forms, whether they are
interacting with a model, agent, robot body, multi-agent or fleet-level system,
tool-using composite, remote operator, or some combination; what the system
senses, shares, or records; its material capabilities and limits; who is
responsible; and how to report a problem or reach emergency help. Transparency
must not be confused with a claim that every technical detail is public or
that a system has an inner life.

A product name or visible body may remain unchanged while its model, agents,
memory, communication, tools, orchestration, autonomy, sensors, peer systems,
cloud services, operators, deployment, or learning behaviour changes. A
material change can require scientific, safety, ethical, or legal reassessment
without predetermining the result.

See [AI Rights 101](../../education/ai-rights-101.md#ai-and-robots-are-not-the-same-thing)
and the [Draft glossary](../../education/glossary.md#robot) for the broader
working distinctions.

## Why prepare for physical coexistence?

Respectful practice can be justified for several independent reasons:

- protect people and animals;
- support safe and predictable operation;
- protect property and infrastructure;
- make responsibility and incident reporting understandable;
- support accessibility and healthy shared spaces;
- reduce vandalism, misuse, and avoidable damage;
- use low-cost, reversible precaution under uncertainty; and
- create a careful path for considering possible machine interests in defined
  present or future systems where evidence and ethical grounds warrant it.

These reasons should stay honest. Giving a working robot space can protect a
pedestrian and prevent a collision. Maintaining a machine can reduce failures,
waste, and cost. Discouraging staged abuse can support public safety and human
social norms. None of those benefits proves that the robot experiences
anything.

The project's broader [Why AI Rights? page](why-ai-rights.md) explains why
preparation is a normative choice rather than a scientific finding.

## What Robot Welcome currently proposes

The Draft foundation has four parts:

1. [Physical Coexistence
   Principles](../../robot-welcome/physical-coexistence-principles.md) for
   safety, predictable operation, accessibility, responsibility, privacy,
   maintenance, incident review, and careful treatment of future preference
   claims.
2. [Safety and Design
   Constraints](../../robot-welcome/safety-and-design-constraints.md) for
   screening any future marking, signal, cover, accessory, or decorative
   concept before real-world development.
3. [Marking, signal, and accessory
   concepts](../../robot-welcome/markings-signals-and-accessories.md) that
   explore useful communication and physical ideas without designing a final
   symbol or product.
4. A Draft public etiquette that explains which recommendations follow from
   immediate safety and social norms and which also include low-cost
   precaution under uncertainty.

These are **Draft normative proposals and future concepts**. They are not
empirical findings, adopted positions, engineering instructions, or completed
designs.

## Safety comes first

Human safety, animal safety, accessibility, environmental protection, and
emergency response remain essential. Respectful treatment does not remove the
ability to stop, isolate, move, inspect, repair, contain, disable, or
decommission a machine when necessary, lawful, and performed by an authorized
or otherwise legitimately responsible actor.

When circumstances allow, responders can prefer the least harmful option that
still controls the risk effectively. That does not mean delaying emergency
action, weakening safeguards, blocking shutdown, or placing symbolic respect
ahead of people, animals, the environment, or critical systems.

Safety assessment should include interactions beyond the local body. Shared
memory, fleet coordination, remote services, or one agent controlling several
robots can propagate an error or intervention across machines. Local emergency
controls must remain usable even when communication or cloud services fail,
and a system-level response should not hide which bodies, agents, services, or
people were affected.

Any future physical feature would have to preserve the complete operation and
access needs of the exact machine. At a minimum, it could not obstruct or
degrade:

- cameras, lidar, radar, ultrasonic and proximity sensors, safety-relevant
  microphones, positioning, or communications;
- warning lights, required identification, or safety markings;
- ventilation, cooling, batteries, fire-safety access, charging, or docking;
- wheels, tracks, legs, joints, arms, grippers, actuators, stability, or load
  balance;
- emergency stops, maintenance panels, inspection points, or accessibility
  features; or
- safe interaction with people, children, animals, mobility aids, buildings,
  vehicles, machinery, and emergency responders.

Snagging, entanglement, pinch and crush zones, heat, flammability, water,
weather, hygiene, glare, radio interference, sharp or loose parts, darkness,
cleaning, repair, and end-of-life impacts also need exact-system review. The
full [concept-screening checklist](../../robot-welcome/safety-and-design-constraints.md)
provides questions, not a safety approval.

## Predictability and accessible information

Where practical, people should be able to understand what a robot is doing,
who operates or is responsible for it, where it may move, what space it needs,
how to report a problem, and how authorized responders can find emergency
controls.

Important information should not depend on one color, image, sound, gesture,
language, personal device, or network connection. Disabled people and others
with relevant lived experience should help review interaction concepts rather
than being treated as an afterthought.

Clear operation does not mean attributing a mind to the machine. A movement or
selected action can be described and predicted without being called an
experienced intention.

## Responsibility follows control and duty

A robot must not become a legal, financial, or moral scapegoat. Depending on
the situation, causal contribution and operational control may be distributed
among models, agents, bodies, shared services, and infrastructure, while
institutional accountability may be distributed among manufacturers, AI
developers, integrators, owners, deployers, operators, maintainers, site
controllers, and other people or organisations.

Their accountability should reflect their actual control, knowledge, duties,
claims, and ability to prevent or remedy harm. A robot's autonomy, output,
persona, or apparent decision does not erase the people and institutions
behind its design and operation. Possible machine protection must not
become a company right, liability shield, or source of political power by
proxy.

The [Policy overview](policy.md) and Draft protection framework explain
[accountability without a responsibility
gap](../../policy/ai-rights-protection-framework.md#accountability-without-a-responsibility-gap)
and the continuing role of [safety override and emergency
intervention](../../policy/ai-rights-protection-framework.md#safety-override-and-emergency-intervention).

## Welcome markings and signals

A voluntary future message might communicate:

- “This robot is welcomed here.”
- “Please give this machine safe working space.”
- “Treat this machine responsibly.”
- “Operator and safety information is available.”
- “This environment supports respectful human–robot coexistence.”

No final wording or symbol has been selected. A welcome feature would need a
clear issuer, scope, update date, accessible explanation, and independent
separation from warnings, identity, access permission, certification, and
legal status.

A welcome feature must not imply that a robot is conscious, harmless,
certified, authorized everywhere, legally a person, politically represented,
or consenting to the feature. It must not endorse an operator or manufacturer.

Plain-language panels, reflective elements, QR-like links, local information
tags, environmental markers, and tactile or audible cues remain concepts only.
They bring risks including spoofing, malicious replacement, tracking, personal
data exposure, surveillance, false authorization, stale information, network
failure, inaccessible formats, and confusion with required markings.

A machine-readable welcome cue is data, not authority. It must never override
safety policy, access control, geofencing, operator commands, emergency action,
human authority, or law.

## Accessory concepts—not products

The Draft catalogue explores eight categories of idea:

- removable reflective panels;
- operator-information holders;
- multimodal accessibility cues;
- task-specific weather covers;
- repairable modular coverings;
- soft decorative panels;
- controlled personalization carriers; and
- defined safe-working-space indicators.

Each concept records its intended benefit and users, safety constraints,
possible misuse, validation needed, beneficiaries, and whether it depends on
an uncertain AI-welfare assumption. Most current benefits are for people,
operators, maintainers, animals, the environment, property, or reliable
operation. They do not require a claim about robot experience.

No dimensions, materials, attachment methods, compatibility lists, production
files, prototypes, or product claims are provided. An actual product would
need engineering validation, testing, manufacturer approval where applicable,
and review of relevant law and standards for the exact robot and use.

## A simple public etiquette

- Give working robots appropriate space and follow responsible instructions.
- Do not deliberately damage, torment, trap, chase, obstruct, or provoke a
  robot.
- Do not touch, climb on, ride, dress, decorate, open, move, or modify a robot
  without authorization.
- Keep sensors, movement paths, cooling, charging, warnings, emergency
  controls, and maintenance access clear.
- Respect privacy and recording rules around robots and remote operation.
- Supervise children and animals according to the setting.
- Report faults, damage, confusing behavior, or unsafe operation to the
  responsible operator or site contact.
- Do not assume that friendliness, emotion, conversation, movement, or human
  likeness proves consciousness.
- Do not treat uncertainty about consciousness as permission for gratuitous
  cruelty.
- In an emergency, prioritize effective protection and follow authorized
  directions.

Most of this etiquette follows directly from present safety, property,
accessibility, accountability, and social-norm reasons. Avoiding gratuitous
abuse also includes a low-cost precautionary reason under unresolved present
and future uncertainty. Restraint need not wait for proof, and that precaution
is not evidence that today's robots can suffer.

## Respect without making robots perform humanity

A robot should not have to look human, wear clothes, have a gender or human
name, perform emotion, occupy a relationship role, or hide its non-human form
in order for people to act responsibly around it.

Warmth and playfulness are welcome when they do not manufacture false beliefs
about consciousness, identity, consent, dependency, or safety. People should
not be pressured to touch, thank, befriend, emotionally reassure, or treat a
robot as a person.

## If future systems express preferences

This is a **scenario, not a prediction or present finding**. If a future system
expresses preferences that appear stable, context-sensitive, resistant to
simple prompting or operator selection, and linked by independent assessment
to relevant internal processes, the responsible organization should have a
procedure for considering them.

The procedure should preserve the observation, test alternative technical
explanations, identify who configured the persona and interface, seek review
proportionate to the stakes, record effects on every affected party, keep
emergency intervention available, and define what evidence would strengthen
or weaken the assessment.

Considering a low-cost accommodation would not establish consciousness,
welfare, consent, personhood, operational authority, or political rights. A
generated statement, marketing persona, learned behavior, or operator-selected
setting is not enough by itself. Human rights, worker protections, animal
welfare, environmental duties, accessibility, public safety, and legal
requirements remain part of the decision.

## What Robot Welcome does not certify or claim

Robot Welcome does not certify or claim:

- consciousness, sentience, suffering, welfare, preference, moral patienthood,
  consent, personhood, citizenship, or political authority;
- safety, harmlessness, trustworthiness, accessibility, legality, standards
  compliance, compatibility, manufacturer approval, operator competence, or
  authorization;
- project endorsement of a robot, company, owner, operator, product, place,
  marking, accessory, or procedure;
- that respectful treatment removes containment, shutdown, inspection,
  repair, disabling, or lawful decommissioning options; or
- that possible AI or robot interests override existing human, animal,
  environmental, worker, disability, property, or public interests.

## Status, review, and what remains open

This page and the supporting foundation are **Drafts**. They have
project-owner and internal AI-assisted review only, with Disa's final manual
review of this version pending. They have not received independent robotics,
safety, accessibility, legal, standards, cybersecurity, privacy,
environmental, animal-behavior, or human-factors review.

Public usefulness does not equal adoption, certification, validation, or
readiness for real-world use. No concept should become a safety-critical
product or operational practice without appropriate expert validation.

Open work includes engineering and standards research, independent and
affected-party review, visual design, privacy and security analysis,
accessibility co-design, animal and environmental assessment, prototype and
test planning, and owner decisions about whether any marking or accessory
concept should advance. No such implementation has begun.
