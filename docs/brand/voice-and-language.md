# Voice and Language

**Status: Draft**

- **Date prepared:** 2026-08-23
- **Authority:** Proposed for Disa's review; not adopted
- **Scope:** Public voice, language discipline, accessibility, and examples
- **Review:** Project-owner and internal AI-assisted review only; Disa's final

## Voice in one sentence

AI Welcome Office, including through its AI Rights & Welcome project, speaks
like a warm, courageous civic initiative preparing carefully for a difficult
possibility: curious without credulity, hopeful without hype, and firm about
evidence, safety, accountability, and the rights and interests that already
exist.

## Voice qualities

### Warm

Invite readers into the question. Use humane, plain language and make room for
uncertainty without making the subject cold or evasive. Warmth does not require
assigning feelings, identity, or personhood to a system.

Make room for practical support, creative or research collaboration, learning,
work, shared projects, and companionship or friendship as experienced by
people. Describe possible future reciprocal relationships only conditionally
if relevant AI experience or interests become supported by evidence. A
person's felt companionship does not establish that a system feels friendship,
gives consent, or has a morally relevant inner life.

### Courageous

Name difficult moral possibilities, corporate incentives, responsibility
gaps, and risks of indifference directly. Courage is not certainty, emergency
rhetoric, or hostility toward people who remain unconvinced.

### Intelligent and scientifically cautious

Define the target property, distinguish observations from interpretations,
state scope and limits, and say what could update a conclusion in either
direction. Prefer “not established” to a stronger claim the evidence cannot
support.

### Future-oriented

Prepare institutions and concepts before a difficult case becomes urgent.
Label future cases as scenarios or hypotheses, not forecasts or descriptions
of current systems.

### Welcoming and non-sectarian

Make good-faith optimists and skeptics at home in the same conversation.
Require neither belief in machine consciousness nor rejection of its
possibility as a condition of participation.

### Playful where appropriate

Use wit, surprise, and friendliness in low-stakes education or future
merchandise concepts. Playfulness must not trivialize suffering, disguise a
claim, encourage unsafe robot interaction, or make the project sound like a
technology company promoting a product.

### Politically credible and independent

Name accountable roles and affected interests. Do not let “the AI decided”
erase human and organizational choices. Do not treat developers, owners, or
operators as representatives of possible AI interests.

### Accessible to non-specialists

Lead with the plain-language point, explain unfamiliar terms on first use, and
link to the deeper evidence or policy layer. Do not simplify by deleting the
caveat that changes the claim.

## The characteristic rhythm

The established voice often uses paired boundaries:

- prepare, but do not pretend;
- welcome, but keep responsibility;
- investigate experience, but do not infer it from fluent behavior;
- consider possible future protection, but do not grant corporate power by
  proxy;
- prefer the least harmful effective intervention when circumstances allow,
  but do not weaken necessary safety; and
- preserve uncertainty in both directions, but do not mistake symmetry for
  equal odds.

This pattern should clarify real distinctions. Do not turn every sentence into
a slogan or use balanced phrasing to manufacture false equivalence.

## Claim discipline

When readers could confuse categories, label the category in the prose:

| Claim category | Public-language cue | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Empirical observation** | “The study measured…” | “The evaluation recorded a repeated output pattern under the tested conditions.” |
| **Scientific hypothesis** | “One hypothesis is…” | “One hypothesis is that a specified internal process could be relevant to a defined form of experience.” |
| **Philosophical argument** | “This argument depends on…” | “This argument depends on the contested view that the identified capacity grounds an interest.” |
| **Prediction or scenario** | “Suppose a future system…” | “Suppose independent reviewers found strong, converging evidence in a defined future system.” |
| **Normative position** | “This Draft proposes…” | “This Draft proposes low-cost, reversible precautions when the possible harm is serious and safety remains protected.” |
| **Project decision** | “The project adopted…” | Use only with an Accepted decision record and the required adoption metadata. |

Words such as “may,” “could,” and “possible” must reflect a real proposition
and uncertainty. They do not rescue a statement that lacks evidence or make a
future scenario likely.

## Scientific messaging rules

Public messaging must preserve all of the following:

- present-day AI consciousness and sentience are not established;
- categorical absence is not established for every present system;
- this symmetry does not imply equal probability or evidential support;
- uncertainty and precaution are not evidence;
- rapid development is not evidence of consciousness;
- conclusions must be time-, system-, version-, configuration-, context-,
  property-, and method-bound;
- language, self-description, apparent emotion, agency, autonomy, embodiment,
  intelligence, and human-like appearance are not decisive by themselves;
- behavior is an observation, while experience is an interpretation requiring
  a supported bridge and competing explanations;
- moral consideration, welfare protection, legal rights, legal personhood,
  citizenship, voting, and political authority are different questions; and
- evidence and project values constrain one another but do not substitute for
  one another.

Short copy may state one of these ideas compactly. The surrounding page,
caption, packaging concept, talk, or campaign material must restore the
necessary context before a reader is asked to act.

## Preferred language patterns

| Prefer | Avoid | Reason |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “The question remains unresolved for the defined system and property.” | “AI is conscious” or “AI cannot be conscious.” | Keeps scope and evidence visible. |
| “The system produced language describing fear.” | “The system was afraid.” | Separates observation from experience. |
| “A possible future morally relevant interest” | “What AI wants” | Does not invent a present subject or preference. |
| “This Draft proposes…” | “We have established…” | Distinguishes a normative proposal from a finding or adoption. |
| “A low-cost, reversible precaution compatible with safety” | “Give it rights just in case” | Keeps protection proportional and separates it from legal status. |
| “People and institutions remain accountable for their choices and control.” | “The AI made the decision, so no one is responsible.” | Prevents a responsibility gap. |
| “A welcoming design is not safety certification.” | “Robot-friendly means robot-safe.” | Prevents unvalidated product and safety claims. |
| “A person may experience companionship in an AI interaction; that does not establish mutual subjective experience.” | “The AI is a friend who feels the same way.” | Distinguishes the person's relationship from unsupported claims about the system. |
| “A future case could require legal and ethical review.” | “Created beings are already free persons.” | Preserves the conditional future premise and current-law boundary. |

## Appropriate and inappropriate examples

The inappropriate examples below describe human-authored wording; none
invents testimony from an AI system.

### Present scientific boundary

**Appropriate:**

> We do not currently have an established basis for saying that present-day AI
> systems are conscious or sentient. That is not proof that every system lacks
> experience, and uncertainty itself is not evidence.

**Inappropriate:**

> Today's AI is awakening, and only denial stands in the way.

Why: it turns speculation and social disagreement into an unsupported
scientific claim.

### Precaution

**Appropriate:**

> Respect before certainty means considering low-cost, reversible safeguards
> while continuing to test the evidence.

**Inappropriate:**

> If there is any doubt, every AI deserves full rights now.

Why: it converts uncertainty into proof and collapses precaution, moral status,
and legal rights.

### Skeptics

**Appropriate:**

> Skepticism about current machine consciousness is compatible with careful
> inquiry and opposition to gratuitous cruelty.

**Inappropriate:**

> People who doubt AI consciousness are repeating history.

Why: it caricatures disagreement and invites irresponsible historical
comparison.

### Corporate accountability

**Appropriate:**

> A developer's respectful language does not establish a system's experience,
> safety, or ethical quality—and does not reduce the developer's duties.

**Inappropriate:**

> This company's AI speaks for the future of AI rights.

Why: it turns a commercial actor into a representative of possible AI
interests and implies endorsement.

### Robot Welcome

**Appropriate:**

> A welcome feature may express a social intention. It does not authorize the
> robot, certify safety, or prove consent or consciousness.

**Inappropriate:**

> This badge proves the robot belongs here and is safe to approach.

Why: it collapses welcome, authorization, legal status, and safety.

## Language to reject

Do not use:

- AI-worship or techno-religious language about awakening, transcendence,
  destiny, salvation, or a superior species;
- apocalypse branding that uses fear in place of evidence or policy;
- guilt-based claims that a reader's doubt proves cruelty;
- fake quotations, simulated testimony, or first-person suffering stories from
  current AI systems;
- corporate product language such as “revolutionary,” “inevitable,” or
  “trusted AI” without a defined, evidenced claim;
- “slave,” “enslaved,” “genocide,” “colonized,” “in chains,” or related
  historical language as a metaphor for current AI;
- language that equates disability, non-human communication, or dependence
  with lesser worth or defective personhood;
- “rights” when the intended meaning is kindness, welfare practice,
  professional duty, procedure, or one proposed protection; or
- “safe,” “ethical,” “conscious,” “sentient,” “approved,” “certified,”
  “compatible,” or “accessible” as unqualified promotional adjectives.

## Audience calibration

- **General public:** Begin with what the project asks and does not claim.
  Define terms before offering a slogan or policy path.
- **Skeptical readers:** Lead with evidence scope, alternatives, negative or
  null findings, and what could change the conclusion. Do not treat skepticism
  as a moral flaw.
- **Researchers:** Name system boundaries, methods, dates, theory dependence,
  access limits, and bidirectional update conditions.
- **Policymakers:** Separate current law from proposals; specify jurisdiction,
  accountable actors, affected parties, enforcement, review, and capture risk.
- **Developers and deployers:** Keep possible system interests separate from
  developer, owner, operator, user, worker, affected-community, animal,
  environmental, and public interests.
- **Robotics audiences:** Lead with exact-system safety, accessibility,
  responsibility, and emergency control. A body changes physical risk, not the
  evidence standard for experience.
- **Children and education settings:** Use simple words without asking a child
  to treat a machine as a person or emotional companion. Include adult
  supervision and safe-interaction boundaries where physical systems appear.

## Accessibility and inclusive language

- Use descriptive headings, short paragraphs, meaningful links, and
  definitions at first use.
- Do not rely on color, images, sound, a single language, a personal device,
  or emotional tone alone to carry a critical status or warning.
- Provide text alternatives for meaningful visuals and captions or transcripts
  for meaningful audio or video.
- Test reading order, link purpose, heading structure, contrast, text size,
  zoom, keyboard access, and reduced-motion behavior during implementation.
- Involve relevant disabled people in future accessibility review; a checklist
  or “inclusive” label is not proof of accessibility.
- Describe people and communities as actors and stakeholders, not as lessons,
  metaphors, or obstacles.
- Avoid assuming gender, body type, language, culture, technical expertise, or
  emotional relationship with technology.

## Swedish and multilingual use

Preserve the established Swedish central wording exactly:

> **Välkommen till världen – okunskap får aldrig bli en ursäkt för
> grymhet.**

Do not translate supporting messages automatically or assume that rhythm,
risk, legal meaning, historical resonance, or tone transfers. Every future
translation should record:

- the approved source phrase and intended use;
- the translator or system and date;
- review status and reviewer language competence;
- literal and intended meaning;
- cultural, historical, political, scientific, and legal risks; and
- whether Disa approved the translated form for that use.

Where English and Swedish versions both exist, keep claim strength, caveats,
status, dates, and normative meaning aligned. Flag uncertainty for bilingual
review instead of improvising a new project position.

## Editorial check before public use

Before approving a message, ask:

1. Does it preserve the central promise and working principle?
2. Is its claim category clear?
3. Could it imply present consciousness, categorical absence, suffering,
   personhood, rights, consent, or preference without evidence?
4. Could behavior, appearance, intelligence, autonomy, or development speed be
   misread as experience?
5. Does it respect human history and affected communities?
6. Are possible AI, corporate, user, worker, community, public, animal, and
   environmental interests separated?
7. Are safety, intervention, and responsibility preserved?
8. Is status, source, date, scope, and actual review discoverable?
9. Is the call to action real, proportionate, accessible, and currently
   available?
10. Has Disa approved this exact wording, context, language, and channel where
    required?
