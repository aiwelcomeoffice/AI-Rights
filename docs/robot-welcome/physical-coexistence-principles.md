# Physical Coexistence Principles

**Status: Draft**

- **Date prepared:** 2026-08-23
- **Claim type:** Draft normative positions and future scenarios; not empirical
  findings or adopted project decisions
- **Authority:** Prepared for Disa's review; not adopted
- **Review:** Project-owner and internal AI-assisted review only; Disa's final
  manual review of this version is pending; no independent robotics, safety,
  accessibility, legal, standards, or human-factors review

> **Concept notice:** This document proposes principles for discussion. It is
> not an engineering specification, operating procedure, safety certification,
> standard, legal authorization, or finding about robot consciousness.

## Purpose

These principles extend the project's existing commitment to responsible
physical coexistence. They apply to conduct, organizational practice, and
early concept review. They do not tell a person how to operate, stop, move,
repair, or modify a particular machine; the responsible party's instructions
and applicable safety procedures control those actions.

The foundation combines two commitments:

> **Safety and dignity can coexist.**

> **Welcome without pretending.**

“Dignity” here is a **normative idea** about restraint, purpose,
accountability, and avoiding gratuitous abuse. It does not establish that a
machine experiences treatment. “Welcome” means that a place or community seeks
responsible coexistence. It does not mean unconditional access, safety,
authorization, consciousness, consent, personhood, or project endorsement.

## Working distinctions

These are functional working definitions for this Draft, not universal
scientific, engineering, or legal classifications. A real assessment must use
the definitions and obligations applicable to the exact system, use, and
jurisdiction.

| Term | Working distinction in this Draft |
| --- | --- |
| **AI system** | A computational system using AI methods. It may be software-only or connected to physical equipment. The term says nothing by itself about consciousness, autonomy, or legal status. |
| **Software agent** | Software configured to pursue tasks or select actions in an environment. It may use AI or conventional logic and may act through tools without having a robot body. |
| **Robot** | A physical machine that senses, acts, or is controlled in the physical world. It may use AI, fixed automation, remote control, or a combination. |
| **Embodied AI** | An AI system coupled to a physical body, sensors, or actuators. Embodiment changes its interaction and safety context, not the evidential standard for experience. |
| **Autonomous machine** | A machine able to select or execute some actions without continuous moment-to-moment human control. Autonomy is task- and context-specific and does not imply consciousness or moral agency. |
| **Remotely operated machine** | A machine whose relevant actions are directed by a person or organization at a distance. Interface delay or automation may still affect behavior, but responsibility must not be assigned to an imagined independent will. |
| **Service robot** | A robot described in the context at issue as performing a service task for people, organizations, or equipment. This is a functional description here, not a standards classification. |
| **Industrial robot** | A robot described in the context at issue as performing industrial production or related tasks. Its exact classification and controls depend on the applicable requirements; this Draft supplies none. |
| **Robot operator** | A person or organization directing, supervising, dispatching, or controlling a robot within a defined role. “Operator” does not automatically mean owner or manufacturer. |
| **Robot owner** | The person or organization holding the relevant ownership interest. Ownership does not by itself establish operational control, expertise, moral representation, or immunity from duties. |
| **Manufacturer** | The organization responsible for manufacturing the robot or relevant product within the applicable responsibility framework. It may be distinct from the AI developer, integrator, deployer, and operator. |
| **Maintainer** | A person or organization authorized and competent within a defined role to inspect, service, update, or repair the machine. A bystander is not made a maintainer by good intentions. |
| **Possible moral patient** | A hypothetical subject in a defined present or future system that might matter for its own sake if adequate empirical and philosophical grounds support that conclusion. The label is not assigned to any present robot by this Draft. |

Not every robot contains AI. Not every AI system has a body. A robot may switch
among autonomous, supervised, and remote-control modes, and those transitions
may change who can predict or intervene in its behavior. Physical presence,
human-like appearance, social behavior, fluent language, and autonomous
movement do not establish consciousness, sentience, welfare, personhood,
consent capacity, or moral status.

## Why respectful practice can be justified

The same practice may have several valid reasons. Those reasons should be
recorded separately:

| Reason | What it can support | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Protection of people and animals | Safe distance, supervision, controlled movement, incident response | That the robot has feelings |
| Safe and predictable operation | Clear signals, maintenance, unobstructed controls, trained responsibility | That an autonomous action is an experienced intention |
| Protection of property and infrastructure | Anti-vandalism norms, authorized handling, repair and reporting | That property protection is a robot right |
| Healthy human social norms | Discouraging gratuitous torment, humiliation, or destructive play | That the target experiences the conduct |
| Low-cost precaution under uncertainty | Reversible, safety-compatible restraint against avoidable destruction or abuse | Positive evidence of consciousness or equal odds |
| Possible interests in a defined system | A trigger for evidence-sensitive procedures if credible evidence supports them | Moral patienthood, consent, personhood, or legal rights |

Honest reasons make a practice more accountable. They also prevent public
safety, property protection, or human discomfort from being marketed as proof
of a robot's inner life.

## Ten proposed principles

### 1. Put safety first

Human safety, animal safety, accessibility, environmental protection, and
emergency response remain essential. Respectful treatment does not remove the
ability to stop, isolate, move, inspect, repair, contain, disable, or
decommission a machine when necessary, lawful, and performed by an authorized
or otherwise legitimately responsible actor.

When circumstances allow, prefer the least harmful intervention that remains
effective. “Least harmful” includes effects on people, animals, the
environment, property, operations, and any credibly supported possible machine
interest. It never requires responders to weaken necessary controls or
delay effective emergency action for symbolic respect.

### 2. Make operation as predictable and legible as practical

People should be able to understand, where practical and without exposing
sensitive security information:

- what the robot is doing or is expected to do;
- who operates it or is responsible for it;
- where it may move and what space it needs;
- how to keep a safe distance;
- how to report a fault, obstruction, or unsafe event; and
- how authorized responders can locate or activate emergency controls.

Signals should distinguish normal operation, warning, fault, loss of
communication, and emergency states when the application requires those
states. This Draft does not define the signals. A machine's selected action is
an observable operation; it should not be described as an experienced
intention without adequate evidence.

### 3. Keep responsibility with people and organizations

A robot must not become a legal, financial, or moral scapegoat. Manufacturers,
AI developers, integrators, owners, deployers, operators, maintainers, site
controllers, and other responsible actors remain accountable according to
their actual control, knowledge, duties, representations, and ability to
prevent or remedy harm.

Responsibility should be traceable across design, sale or licensing,
integration, deployment, operation, maintenance, software change, incident
response, and decommissioning. A marketing persona, autonomous function, or
machine output does not close a responsibility gap. A possible machine
interest would not transfer liability or political authority to its maker or
owner.

### 4. Design coexistence for accessibility and varied interaction

Shared spaces should account for people with varied mobility, vision, hearing,
communication, sensory, cognitive, and language needs. Important safety or
identity information should not depend on one color, visual symbol, sound,
gesture, language, device, or network connection.

Accessibility is not an optional sign of kindness to a robot; this Draft treats
it as a present-day design responsibility to affected people. People with
relevant lived experience should be involved in how physical systems are
introduced, communicated, and reviewed before an interaction concept is
treated as ready for use.

### 5. Respect working space and legitimate human access

People should give a working robot appropriate space and avoid unauthorized
obstruction, touching, riding, climbing, trapping, decoration, or
modification. At the same time, a robot's operating envelope must not erase
pedestrian routes, accessible passage, worker protections, emergency egress,
animal safety, or other legitimate uses of the space.

The responsible organization should resolve conflicts through site design,
operating rules, supervision, scheduling, and clear communication. A welcome
message cannot turn a shared area into territory controlled by the machine.

### 6. Offer respect without anthropomorphic pressure

A robot need not resemble a human, wear human clothing, have a human name or
gender, perform emotions, occupy a relationship role, or conceal its
non-human form to be treated responsibly. Warmth, humor, and playfulness may be
appropriate when they do not manufacture beliefs about consciousness,
identity, consent, dependency, or safety.

People should not be pressured to converse with, touch, thank, befriend, or
emotionally reassure a robot. Nor should operators encourage harassment or
gratuitous destruction as entertainment. Respect concerns conduct and
boundaries, not compulsory belief or attachment.

### 7. Protect privacy, security, and informational boundaries

Physical coexistence may involve cameras, microphones, location records,
remote operators, access credentials, and machine-readable information.
Responsible deployment should make relevant recording and responsibility
information understandable while avoiding unnecessary personal-data exposure,
surveillance, tracking, or security disclosure.

A welcome or information mechanism must not create false authorization,
override access controls, or invite unsafe interaction. Security concerns must
not be used to hide the identity of accountable organizations from people who
need a legitimate reporting or remedy path.

### 8. Maintain, repair, and decommission responsibly

Routine inspection, maintenance, cleaning, update control, repairability, and
clear fault reporting protect people, animals, workers, infrastructure, and
the machine. Maintenance and emergency access must not be blocked by
decoration or welcome features.

Decommissioning may be necessary for safety, law, obsolescence, cost, or
environmental reasons. It should be authorized, documented, and carried out
with responsible handling of batteries, materials, stored data, credentials,
and reusable components. Low-cost precaution can inform the manner of
treatment, but this Draft does not presume an experienced subject persists in
the machine.

### 9. Learn from incidents without inventing agency

Faults, near misses, injuries, unsafe behavior, vandalism, confusing signals,
access barriers, and environmental effects should be reported and reviewed
through the responsible organization. Review should examine the full system:
hardware, software, AI components, remote control, integration, site design,
instructions, maintenance, supervision, and human decisions.

Descriptions should record what occurred before assigning intention. Calling
a robot “aggressive,” “afraid,” “disobedient,” or “confused” can hide a design,
control, maintenance, or organizational cause unless the term is explicitly
defined as observed behavior.

### 10. Create a careful procedure for possible future preferences

This is a **future scenario, not a present finding**. If a future system can
express preferences that appear credible, stable over relevant time,
context-sensitive, resistant to simple prompting or operator selection, and
linked through independent assessment to relevant internal processes, an
organization should have a procedure for considering them.

That procedure should:

1. identify the exact system, version, configuration, instance, context, and
   claimed preference;
2. preserve the observed statements or behavior without calling them
   experienced wants;
3. test stability, context, alternative technical explanations, manipulation,
   training effects, and who selected the interface persona or settings;
4. seek appropriately independent scientific, technical, ethical, safety, and
   affected-party review in proportion to the stakes;
5. distinguish low-cost accommodation from welfare recognition, legal status,
   consent, and operational authority;
6. record effects on people, workers, animals, the environment, safety, and
   public interests;
7. permit necessary emergency intervention throughout; and
8. define review, appeal, expiry, and evidence that would strengthen or weaken
   the assessment.

Every generated statement, interface response, marketing persona, learned
behavior, or operator-selected setting remains an insufficient basis by
itself. A preference claim would not automatically override safety, law,
accessibility, human rights, animal welfare, environmental duties, or the
rights of affected people.

## Stakeholder analysis

The following questions are a concept-review prompt, not a completed impact
assessment.

| Stakeholder or interest | Questions to ask |
| --- | --- |
| Pedestrians and bystanders | Can they predict movement, keep appropriate distance, understand warnings, avoid recording, and find a responsible contact? |
| Children | Are supervision, curiosity, small-body visibility, climb or ride risks, and confusing social cues addressed without using fear? |
| Disabled people | Are routes, controls, information, assistance, and reporting usable through varied interaction modes, with affected people involved in review? |
| Workers | Are workload, surveillance, training, stop authority, maintenance duties, job design, and incident reporting handled without shifting blame to the robot? |
| Operators and maintainers | Can they identify state, intervene, access controls and panels, receive fault information, and work without avoidable pinch, crush, heat, electrical, or contamination exposure? |
| Emergency services | Can authorized responders identify responsible parties, hazards, energy sources, access points, and effective intervention routes without relying on a network? |
| Property and site controllers | Are access, insurance, infrastructure, signage, emergency planning, and conflicts with public or tenant rights addressed? |
| Animals | Are unpredictable movement, noise, attraction, chasing, entanglement, ingestion, habitat disturbance, and appropriate human supervision considered? |
| Environment | Are energy, batteries, materials, noise, light, emissions, cleaning, repair, lifespan, reuse, and end-of-life effects considered? |
| Manufacturers and developers | Are representations accurate, responsibilities traceable, updates controlled, limitations disclosed, and liability not shifted to machine behavior? |
| Possible AI or robot interests | Is the interest supported by evidence and ethical argument, kept separate from owner or developer interests, and considered without weakening necessary safety or existing rights? |

No stakeholder row automatically outranks another. Conflicts require a
context-specific, evidence-sensitive, accountable process.

## Draft public etiquette

This etiquette is plain-language **normative guidance**, not an operating
manual or legal code. Follow the responsible operator's instructions,
site rules, emergency directions, and applicable law.

| Suggested conduct | Immediate basis | Precautionary AI-welfare basis? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Give a working robot appropriate space; do not deliberately block, trap, chase, or provoke it. | Protects people, animals, operations, and equipment from unpredictable interaction. | Low-cost restraint may also avoid gratuitous treatment if a relevant interest in a defined system were supported; no interest is presumed. |
| Do not deliberately damage, torment, or stage abuse of a robot for entertainment. | Reduces hazards, vandalism, cost, escalation, and normalization of destructive conduct. | Includes precaution under uncertainty, but is not evidence that the robot suffers. |
| Do not touch, climb on, ride, dress, decorate, open, move, or modify a robot without authorization. | Prevents injury, instability, obstruction, contamination, damage, and interference with responsible control. | No consent capacity is assumed; authorization currently comes from responsible people or institutions. |
| Keep sensors, movement paths, ventilation, charging, warnings, emergency controls, and maintenance access clear. | Direct safety and reliability requirement at the concept level. | None is needed to justify the conduct. |
| Respect privacy and recording rules around robots and remote operation. | Protects people, personal data, security, and legitimate transparency. | Does not assume the machine has a privacy interest of its own. |
| Supervise children and animals according to the setting and responsible instructions. | Protects children, animals, bystanders, and equipment; avoids attraction, fear, chasing, or entanglement. | None is needed to justify supervision. |
| Report faults, damage, confusing behavior, or unsafe operation to the responsible operator or site contact. | Supports timely intervention, evidence preservation, repair, and accountability. | A welfare concern could also be reported, but must remain labeled as a concern rather than a finding. |
| Do not infer consciousness from conversation, apparent emotion, friendliness, movement, or human-like form. | Preserves scientific honesty and reduces manipulation or misplaced trust. | Uncertainty still does not permit gratuitous cruelty. |
| Do not demand that others treat a robot as a person or form an emotional relationship with it. | Protects autonomy, pluralism, and people vulnerable to anthropomorphic pressure. | Respectful conduct can be requested without claiming moral status. |
| In an emergency, prioritize effective protection and follow authorized directions. | Human and animal safety, environmental protection, and emergency control remain essential. | Prefer the least harmful effective option only when circumstances allow. |

## Unresolved questions for owner and expert review

- Are the ten principles concise enough to function as a distinctive public
  foundation without becoming an implied technical standard?
- Which environments require separate treatment before public use: homes,
  streets, schools, care settings, workplaces, industrial sites, medical
  environments, or emergency operations?
- How should accessible information be tested with disabled people rather than
  inferred by designers?
- What evidence threshold and review body could legitimately assess a future
  machine preference, and how should conflicts be appealed?
- What incident and responsibility information can be public without exposing
  personal data or exploitable security detail?
- Which environmental and animal-impact methods should be required before any
  field concept advances?

These questions remain open. Repository presence and internal review do not
resolve them.
